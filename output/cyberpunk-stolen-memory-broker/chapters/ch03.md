# Chapter 3 — Seventy-Two Seconds of Cable

Ratri Sundoro set the calibration bench on the pontoon at 22:00, when the pumps took the grid, and stated her terms before she opened the case.

"I certify generation and timestamp. Nothing else. An assay counts brains a file has crossed. It cannot tell me whose life it was." She looked up. "So it has to come out of your port at generation one. Second-hand, it proves nothing and I'll say so."

Nayla had the numbers already. Bench, 140 W. Export head, 90 W. Deck lamp, 30 W: two hundred sixty, under the five-hundred ceiling, on a 4 kWh cell that would carry it past dawn with margin. The transcode rig sat dark under its cover, drawing 900 W when it drew anything, useless tonight and useless to her. She was not copying anything into anyone. She was getting one thing out.

Ilham Basri came aboard at 23:40 to stop it and stayed to explain it, which was worse.

"I kept your connectome map," he said. "From the chair, nine years ago. Nobody thought that strange." He did not sit. "I wrote the thermal-fault protocol. Seventy-eight minutes of chair, four hard-shuts at ninety seconds, seventy-two minutes of cable. Four times. Twenty-four recorded hours." A pause. "I copied your hand into the log because yours was the only hand the log ever held."

"Maas."

"One hundred ninety-four gigabytes of drive stock at the bottom of the Ledge channel. He'd take first generation instead of fuel cells." He looked at the water. "I had nothing else at first generation."

The courier boat came alongside at 02:20, tidal speed, no hurry in it. Juno Halim climbed up with the certificate in a sleeve and handed it over without being asked. The markers had finished decaying. The lot re-flagged in her own skull as somebody else's four days ago, and she had spent those days deciding she would rather be nobody than be Nayla.

Ratri read the certificate, then her own bench log. "Generation one, detail 0.94," she said. "My bench says two and 0.88. That is forged provenance. That is fraud, and Maas's file is in the chain now."

At 03:57 Nayla plugged her own mastoid port, alone, and let it run. Thirty-six megabytes at half a megabyte a second. Seventy-two seconds: cable tension behind the ear, a rig fan, Ilham's hands.

The siren fired at 04:00 and the ninety-day edge took the original mid-breath.

Ilham did not run. The crime had never been in the market.

Nayla wrote the log in her own handwriting — time, megabytes, seconds of cable, no charge — and this time it was true. Everything before that hour was somebody else's copy now, twelve per cent thinner, and nothing would ever put it back.
