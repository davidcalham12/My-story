# Chapter 1 — Forty-One Seconds

Noor Adeyemi woke at 15:20 into the tail of the blackout, which was the only part of a magnetopause passage that ever felt like weather. Five hours predicted, five hours delivered, the ionosphere shrugging under the solar wind while the log room hissed with nothing. She lay a moment in the bunk alcove and listened to the heat exchanger, which was the loudest thing at Shoal Point, and to the methane drizzle on the roof, which was the second.

The alternation had slipped again. She could tell before she looked, because the room was warm in the way that meant the heaters had been given the whole of the two radioisotope units for longer than their forty minutes. Six hundred watts electrical, continuous, plus whatever the genset's oxidiser would bear, and the radar's forty kilowatts of pulse came out of the genset or came out of nothing. She threw the alternation back by hand at the panel, radar up, heaters down, and stood in her liner suit with her arms crossed while the pulse count climbed and steadied.

Then the tally sheet, because the checklist said tally sheet.

Oxidiser at six percent. She wrote it in the column in her own small upright hand, and the line above hers was still Emlen Pryce's, sixteen months old, a figure and then a annotation and then a smaller annotation on the annotation, the ink of a man who had never met a number he trusted to stand alone. The Authority had never sent a fresh sheet. She had stopped expecting one.

At 15:40 the first clear relay pass came over and the cache emptied itself into Beacon Eleven.

In the blind half nothing reached the surface unrelayed; for eight days out of every sixteen, Titan's locked face turned away and everything she knew arrived as stored packets from the Relay Trust's satellites. So she was used to traffic arriving in a lump. She was not used to the inbound alert, and she was not used to what came out of the speaker when she crossed the room and let it play.

It was a distress call. It carried Beacon Eleven's station hash — her hash, her pulse signature, the Authority's proof of presence.

And it was her voice. Not like her voice. Hers: flat, the phrase-bank cadence of a commissioning booth at Enceladus, the numerals clipped and spaced the way a machine spaces numerals it has been handed one at a time. It gave a position report. She had never made that position report in her life.

She looked at the clock. Then she looked at the clock again, because she trusted the clock further than she trusted herself, and the packet timestamp had not changed: one hour and eighteen minutes ahead of station time.

The station had already acknowledged it. Automatically, at receipt, per stored procedure, and forwarded it on, before she had finished crossing four metres of floor.

She sat down and logged it verbatim, with the local timestamp, exactly as the Convention required, and added no interpretation, because interpretation was not hers to add. Then she wrote one further line: that she could not power down the beacon to think about it. A lit beacon was not hers to darken. Abandonment was tried on Enceladus, on evidence months late.

Outside, the drizzle went on. The heat exchanger went on. She sat with her own voice on the log and waited for the next pass.
