# Chapter 2 — Divide by Seventy-Eight

The second copy came down at 04:44 on Contract Day 423, and it was not a second copy. It was the same one. Same hash, same waveform, her voice saying *Beacon Eleven, keeper reports* with the flat booth cadence of a woman reading phrases off a card in a room on Enceladus. Only the stamp had moved: 2:36 ahead of station time now, where yesterday it had been 1:18.

She wrote it out verbatim, because that was the law, and then she wrote the two offsets in a column in the margin, which was not.

The third arrived on the pass after that, at 3:54. Three numbers, evenly spaced. She did the subtraction twice and got seventy-eight both times, and sat with the pen not moving, because seventy-eight was not an arbitrary number on Titan this season. Seventy-eight minutes was Earth, one way. It was the number printed on the header of every packet she had sent in fourteen months.

The relay was adding the Earth correction. Then handling the packet again and adding it again. Nothing in the firmware said *already done*; nothing in the firmware needed to, until a packet came round twice. The offset was not a timestamp at all. It was a count of how many times the cache had touched the thing.

Which left the other half of it, and the other half was worse. The cache ran six hours deep. Nothing could loiter in it from one day's pass to the next — the packet should have aged out and died before breakfast on 422. It had not aged out because it had never sat still. Her own station had taken it in as an inbound distress and done what the Convention required of a keeper: log verbatim, acknowledge, forward. The forwarded copy dropped straight back into the cache as a fresh arrival, with a fresh arrival time, and six hours of depth can never expire a thing that keeps arriving. Beacon Eleven was not haunted. Beacon Eleven was renewing the subscription, every pass, by the book.

So she divided. Three cycles at seventy-eight minutes, counted back across the passes, and the arithmetic set her down at 04:12 on Contract Day 422, eleven hours before she had ever heard the call.

The power log was in the drawer where the power log always was. She ran her finger down the alternation column to 04:12 and found the slip she had already corrected and already forgotten: radar unpowered, forty-one seconds, heaters holding. Forty-one seconds of Beacon Eleven not pulsing. The dead-man watchdog had done exactly what it was built to do — spoken a position report in the keeper's voice, because a keeper who cannot speak still has a voice on file, 312 phrases and a numeral set, recorded in ninety minutes and never thought about since.

The call from her future was a receipt for a gap she had slept through.

She sent the query up to Jun Halloran at the Relay Trust anyway, and a pass and a half later the answer came back cached and stale: cache behaviour was proprietary; Rev 6.2 had operated to specification for nineteen years. It read like a lie. She decided it was a muzzle, which was worse, because a muzzle meant he had nothing to add.

The archive locker was cold and smelled of dry ice-dust. Pryce's final nine entries were in his own hand, annotated in his own margins, the way a man annotates himself when there is nobody else to check him.

*1:18.* Then *2:36.* Then *3:54.* Then, two entries later, without emphasis: *The cache keeps things.*

The ninth entry logged an inbound distress. Keeper down, coordinates on the ice apron. He had acknowledged it, because the Convention said acknowledge, and noted the suit check, and the time he went out.

Somebody else's handwriting closed the log nine days later. Iver Brekke's abandonment verdict was filed behind it in the same folder, correct on every page, and wrong.
