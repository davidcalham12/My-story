# Outline — *The Beginning After the End*

## Acts

**Act I — Thursday, the arrival.** Ruth Vance walks in over the bridge with a jar and is fed at the Learys'. The valley's one argument — feed ourselves, or be somewhere people arrive — puts its hands on her before she has set down her pack. Martha Gale does not refuse her; she refuses to sign, which is worse and slower.

**Act II — Saturday dawn, the row already standing.** Ruth has planted before asking. Tom Leary finds the unentered row while clearing the well pipe and does not pull it up. The book's real question stops being whether Ruth is allowed to stay and becomes what Tom will ask for having let it stand.

**Act III — Sunday, the meeting.** Two signatures, one ledger line, one row. Martha signs — not for Ruth, and not against her, but for the boy she has been keeping a row under her own name for. The walnut ground stays walnut. The stranger gets one row of the flats and her jar goes on the shelf.

## Promises

- **Does Ruth Vance get her row, and what does she give for it?** — asked in Chapter 1 when she sets the jar on the Learys' table without opening it; paid off in Chapter 3 when the jar is entered into the store and her name goes in the ledger beside the row already growing.
- **What is in the jar?** — teased in Chapter 1, half-answered in Chapter 2 at the well; answered plainly in Chapter 3 when Martha reads the label and recognises it as something Harrow Bench had and lost. No mystery about the quiet is opened; this is the only thing the book withholds.
- **Why does Tom Leary leave a row he is required by rule to pull up?** — the row is found in Chapter 2 and the reason withheld there; paid off in Chapter 3 when he says it out loud at the meeting and asks for his own line in the ledger.
- **Will Martha Gale sign for a stranger again, after last autumn?** — established in Chapter 1 as a thing she has not done in a year; turned in Chapter 2 when she says why to the one person allowed to be asked; paid off in Chapter 3 when she signs, and for whom.
- **What is Harrow Bench for?** — Owen Pike's argument against Martha's, carried in all three chapters, settled in Chapter 3 not by anyone winning but by the meeting doing both things at once: walnut ground kept, one row of the flats given away.

## Chapters

### Chapter 1 — Fed at the First House

- **POV:** Ruth Vance
- **Where and when:** The Learys' kitchen and the yard outside it, Thursday evening, dusk into dark. First house over the stone bridge. Woodsmoke, wet stone, a pot already on because someone saw her on the road an hour before she reached it.
- **Tension:** 4/10
- **Promise advanced:** Does Ruth get her row and what does she give for it; what is in the jar; will Martha sign again.
- **Beats:**
  - Ruth comes off the bridge road with a pack and a stoppered jar, and is fed before she is asked a single thing — bread, apple, well water — because that is the rule and nobody makes a ceremony of it. She sets the jar on the table beside her plate and does not open it. Tom Leary, eighteen, looks at the jar twice and says nothing about it.
  - Owen Pike arrives while she is still eating, having aired an inn bed for her before she knew she needed one, and talks her case at her rather than to her: three nights, then Sunday, then the meeting decides. He explains the ledger — no money here, the ledger *is* the money — and says, cheerfully and too early, that he wants her name in it by Sunday.
  - Ruth asks the one question she has planned: whether the flats above the upper well are spoken for. Tom answers, because Tom is the one person in the valley people are allowed to ask anything — he was born the spring after, and answers with what he was told. He tells her the pipe runs without carrying and the flats are where anything grown for trade has to go. He does not tell her they are the most argued-over ground on the shelf.
  - Martha Gale comes to the door but not through it. She is polite and entirely unmoved. She says the store opens after the meeting, never before, never by one person, two names and both sign. She does not say no to Ruth. She says she has not signed for a stranger since last autumn, and leaves the reason on the step with her.
  - Alone in the yard afterwards, Ruth turns the jar in her hands and looks up at the dark line of the flats. The chapter ends with her deciding something she does not announce.
- **Carries:** the low, domestic tension of being fed generously by people who have not decided about you yet. No threat, no hostility; only a clock — three nights — and a door that opens on two signatures and has one of them missing.
- **Must leave open:** what is in the jar; what Martha's reason from last autumn actually is; what Ruth decided in the yard.
- **Must close:** the arrival itself. By the end of the chapter the rules are on the page — three nights, two signatures, no unentered rows — stated plainly and by the people they belong to.

### Chapter 2 — What the Pipe Turned Up

- **POV:** Tom Leary
- **Where and when:** The upper well and the flats below it, Saturday before first light into full dawn; then Martha Gale's doorstep an hour later. Frost on the pipe, the valley not awake yet.
- **Tension:** 6/10
- **Promise advanced:** Why Tom leaves the row standing; what is in the jar; will Martha sign again.
- **Beats:**
  - Tom is out early clearing the upper well pipe, work he does alone and has done since he was twelve — a job that is his and is not written anywhere as his, because the row it waters is entered under his mother's name. He gets the blockage out and the water runs, and he follows the wet line down out of habit.
  - He finds the row. Short, eight or nine feet, set in the good corner of the flats, drilled properly and firmed with a boot, watered from his pipe. Unentered. The rule is one sentence long and he has known it since he could read: an unentered row is pulled up by whoever finds it. He crouches at the end of it for a long time and does not put a hand on it.
  - Ruth comes up the path with an empty can, having expected to be caught and not having hurried. She does not apologise and does not explain herself past what is true: she plants before she asks, because a row you can see is an argument and a jar is only a promise. Tom asks what she put in. She tells him — the name of it, and that Harrow Bench used to have it and lost it, and that she has enough for one row and no more. Tom has never seen it grow. He has only been told about it, which is the only way he knows anything from before.
  - Tom goes to Martha Gale's door with wet boots and asks her — not about the row, he says nothing about the row — why she stopped signing. Martha tells him straight: last autumn's walker wintered well, was fed, entered, given a bed and a row, and left in spring with a jar of Harrow seed that has not come back. She has been holding the walnut ground since, and she is honest that the holding is part grief and part spite and she cannot always tell which. It is the longest she has spoken to him in a year.
  - Tom walks home past the flats in full light. The row is still standing. He has not pulled it up and he has not reported it, and he understands that by dawn tomorrow both of those will be something he has to say out loud in front of everyone. He starts working out what he is going to ask for.
- **Carries:** the highest pressure in the book, and all of it internal — a boy alone with a rule he has broken by doing nothing. Nobody raises a voice. Nobody finds out yet.
- **Must leave open:** what Tom will ask for at the meeting; whether Martha will sign and for whom.
- **Must close:** the jar. By the end of this chapter the reader knows what Ruth carried — something the valley once had and lost — and knows Tom saw the row and left it.

### Chapter 3 — Sunday, Two Names

- **POV:** Martha Gale
- **Where and when:** The chapel, Sunday morning, the fourth day. Forty-odd people on cold benches, the vestry door shut behind Martha's back, the ledger open on the table through the wall.
- **Tension:** 5/10, easing to 3/10 by the close.
- **Promise advanced:** All five. Every open question is settled here.
- **Beats:**
  - The meeting opens slow and domestic — the bridge road, a slate off the inn roof, somebody's dog — and Martha sits through it knowing what is coming and already tired of it. Owen Pike makes Ruth's case well and too warmly: three nights, fed, no trouble, and a valley nobody arrives in has agreed to end. Martha gives her own answer without heat, because she has given it before: a tree planted for a stranger is a tree the valley does not eat from, and she is not signing.
  - Tom Leary stands up. He tells the room he found an unentered row on the flats at dawn yesterday, watered off his own pipe, and that he did not pull it up. He says the rule and he says he broke it. Then he asks for what he wants, which is not forgiveness: he wants his own line in the ledger, in his own name, instead of a row kept under his mother's. The room goes quiet in the particular way it does when a rule made eleven winters ago meets the first person born after.
  - Ruth Vance says the only thing she has to say, and says it short: what is in the jar, what the valley used to grow and stopped, that she has enough for one row, and that she will hand the jar to the store whether or not the row is hers. She does not argue for herself past that. She has already planted; the argument is standing on the flats where anyone can walk up and look at it.
  - Martha goes into the vestry, which is where she thinks. She looks at the walnut ground on the map in her head and at the shelves she has not opened for a stranger in a year, and she understands that she has been holding the ground for a boy who was never going to be given a line while she held it. She comes back and signs — and says who the second name should be, and it is Tom's. The store is opened by two names, both signing, as the rule requires and always did.
  - The close is small and unceremonious. Ruth's name goes in the ledger beside the row that was already growing; her jar goes onto the vestry shelf, labelled in Martha's hand. The walnut ground is not given up — it stays walnut, and Martha says so, and nobody argues. Afterwards people go out to do ordinary things with their hands. Tom walks up to clear the pipe, which is now watering a row with his own name at the top of it, and the last image is the short green line on the flats with the whole valley not particularly watching it.
- **Carries:** the deliberate trough after Chapter 2. The pressure comes off across the chapter; the resolution is administrative, and that is the point — a signature, a row of seed, a vote at a Sunday meeting.
- **Must close:** the jar's contents, publicly; Tom's reason and his line in the ledger; Martha's signature and what happens to the walnut ground; whether Ruth stays; what Harrow Bench is for, answered by doing both things at once.
- **Must not introduce:** anything new. No revelation about the quiet, no arrival, no reversal after the signing. The book ends on a row growing and people busy with something else.
