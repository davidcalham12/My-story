# Chapter 1 — Fed at the First House

The bridge was stone and the road past it was not mended, and Ruth Vance came off it into woodsmoke at dusk.

The first house had a lamp in the window and a pot already on. A woman opened the door before she knocked, looked at her boots and not her face, and said, "Sit down, it's nearly ready."

So she sat. Bread, a cut apple, a cup of well water so cold it made her teeth ache. Nobody asked her name. She set the jar on the table beside her plate, stoppered, and left her hand near it. A young man at the hearth — eighteen or so, sleeves pushed up, wet to the elbow from something — looked at the jar, and looked at it again, and said nothing about it.

A man came in without knocking while she was still eating.

"Owen Pike," he said, to the room. "I've aired you a bed at the inn. Don't argue, it was aired anyway." He pulled out a chair and turned it round and sat on it backwards. "Three nights. That's the rule, that's not me. Thursday, Friday, Saturday, and Sunday morning the meeting decides whether you stay. There's no money here. There's a ledger, and the ledger is the money — names, rows, debts of work. I'd like yours in it by Sunday."

"You don't know anything about me," Ruth said.

"No," Owen agreed, pleased. "That's Sunday's problem."

She finished the apple. Then she asked the thing she had carried up the road all afternoon. "The flats above the upper well. Are they spoken for?"

The young man at the hearth answered. "The pipe runs without carrying," he said. "You don't haul to the flats. That's why anything grown for trade goes there." He dried his hands. "Tom Leary. You can ask me things."

"Why you?"

"Born the spring after. I only know what I was told."

The door opened again and a woman stood in it, not coming through. Grey hair, coat still buttoned.

"Martha Gale," she said. "Seed store's in the vestry. It opens after the meeting, never before, and never by one person — two names in the room, both sign." She said it the way you'd say the well was forty feet. "I'm not saying no to you."

"You're not saying yes."

"I haven't signed for a stranger since last autumn," Martha said, and left it there on the step, and went.

Afterwards Ruth stood in the yard with the jar in both hands, turning it slowly, feeling the seed shift inside. Above the roofline the flats lay dark and level against a darker hill.

Three nights.

She put the jar in her coat and went in to sleep.
