# Chapter 2 — What the Pipe Turned Up

The frost had got into the pipe collar, and Tom Leary worked it loose with his thumbs because a tool would have cracked it. Six years of this. The blockage came out as a wad of leaf mould and one flat stone he could not account for, and then the water went through with a knock he felt in the wood of the trough.

He followed the wet line down because his boots always did.

The row was in the good corner of the flats. Eight feet, nine. Drilled straight, the soil firmed along it with the side of a boot, and dark all down its length where somebody had carried water to it from his pipe. Not a mark in the ledger anywhere with that row against it.

He crouched at the end of it. An unentered row is pulled up by whoever finds it. He had known the sentence since he could read. He put his hands on his knees and left them there.

Ruth Vance came up the path with an empty can, not hurrying.

"I don't apologise for it," she said. "A row you can see is an argument. A jar is only a promise."

"What's in it?"

"Skirret." She set the can down. "You had it here. It went out of the valley — before me, before you. I've enough for one row and nothing over."

He had never seen it grow. He had only been told about it, which was how he knew everything that came from before.

An hour later he stood on Martha Gale's step with his boots still wet and asked her why she had stopped signing.

She did not ask why he wanted to know. "Last autumn's walker," she said. "We fed him, entered him, gave him a bed and a row. He wintered well. He went out in spring with a jar of Harrow seed and the jar has not come back." She looked past him at the frost going off the wall. "I've held the walnut ground since. Part of that's grief and part of it's spite and I can't always tell you which is which on a given morning."

It was the longest she had spoken to him in a year.

He walked home past the flats in the full light. The row was still standing, greenless, a line of firmed earth in the corner where the water ran best. He had not pulled it up. He had not told anyone. By tomorrow's dawn both of those would be things he had to say out loud, in the chapel, in front of everybody.

So he began, slowly, working out what he would ask for in return.
