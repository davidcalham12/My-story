# Chapter 3 — Sunday, Two Names

The meeting began with a slate. It had come off the inn roof in the week's wind, and Owen Pike wanted two hands on Tuesday to put it back, and got them before he finished asking. Then the bridge road, which was clear. Then somebody's dog and somebody's hens, settled the way such things settle, with nobody much minding.

Martha Gale sat on the cold end of the second bench and waited. Behind her the vestry door was shut, and through the wall the ledger lay open on the table where it always lay.

Owen made his case warmly, which was his fault and his use. Three nights. Fed at the Learys'. No trouble, no taking. A valley nobody arrives in, he said, has agreed to end.

"A tree planted for a stranger," Martha said, "is a tree we don't eat from. I'm not signing." She said it without heat. She had said it before.

Tom Leary stood up.

"I found a row on the flats yesterday at dawn," he said. "Unentered. Watered off my pipe. The rule says I pull it up." He looked at the window. "I didn't."

Nobody spoke.

"I'm not asking to be forgiven. I want my own line in the ledger. My name, not my mother's."

Ruth Vance said her piece short. Skirret, she said. The valley grew it once and stopped. Enough in the jar for one row, and the jar goes to the store whether the row is hers or not. Then she sat down. The argument was standing on the flats, and anyone could walk up and look at it.

Martha went into the vestry, because that was where she thought. Walnut ground in her head, laid out as she had held it since last autumn. Shelves she had not opened for a stranger in a year. She had been keeping ground for a boy who would never get a line while she kept it.

She came back and said she would sign, and that the second name should be Tom's.

So the store was opened by two names, both signing, as the rule required and always had. Ruth's name went into the ledger beside the row already in the ground. The jar went onto the shelf, labelled in Martha's hand, small letters, the date under it.

"The walnut ground stays walnut," Martha said, and nobody argued.

Then people went out to do ordinary things with their hands. Tom walked up to clear the pipe that watered a row with his own name at the top of it. Down on the flats the short line of firmed earth lay waiting, and the valley was not particularly watching.
