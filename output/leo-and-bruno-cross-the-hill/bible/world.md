# Leo and Bruno Cross the Hill

Leo's village sits in a valley where one particular hill marks the edge of his known world. On the near side: his house with its cardboard observatory in the garden, the lane where the neighborhood children gather, the beach at Cadaques where treasures wash up. On the far side: unknown territory that has always been there, a fold in the landscape he can see but has never reached. This summer, when Leo turns ten, the crossing becomes possible.

## Factions

- **The Household** — Leo's family and immediate world (his garden, his collections, his bed) want things to stay knowable and safe, though they encourage curiosity. They set the limits of permission and notice when he has been gone.
- **The Neighborhood** — the children who gather at the bottom of the lane want adventure and dare, and they know the hill's near slopes by heart. They determine what is socially possible and what is dared into being.
- **The Far Side** — whatever lives beyond the hill (farms, other settlements, forest, different geology) has its own logic and inhabitants. It is genuinely unknown and operates by rules Leo has not learned yet.
- **The Natural World** — weather, darkness, stone, geology, and distance. These work by their own uncompromising rules and do not negotiate with ten-year-old plans.

## Means

- **The Cardboard Observatory** — built with Grandfather; shows stars but only works at night and only when weather permits. Cannot reveal what is too far away to magnify into meaning.
- **Captain Crunch** — the fossil Leo found at the beach, named and kept. A physical proof that other ages existed in the same place; fits in a pocket and weighs what it weighs.
- **The Torch** — has batteries that are tired; casts light for a distance but drains with use. The darkness it illuminates is always larger than the beam.
- **The Bicycle** — Leo's independence and his reach, but it has limits: tire punctures are possible, steep grades test the rider, and the frame cannot carry everything he might wish to bring.
- **Smooth Stones** — Leo's collection from the beach, kept in a jar or pocket. Each one is a small truth about geology and water; together they are a record of patient observation.
- **The Packed Lunch** — supplies that can run out; marks the boundary between venture and stranding, and the distance he can reasonably go and still return before dark.

## Rules

- **R1.** Darkness falls at a fixed time each night; if Leo is not home before then, he has a genuine problem. His fear of the dark is not irrational; it is a correct reading of real limits.
- **R2.** The dog (Bruno) will not leave Leo, but the dog also will not go forward into situations that genuinely terrify him. The dog's behavior is a hard limit on how far recklessness can extend.
- **R3.** Adults (parents, neighbors, maybe a farmer on the far side) notice when children are absent, and they have different ideas about what is safe. Permission to cross the hill is not the same as permission to vanish.
- **R4.** The hill's far side is genuinely different from the near side — different plants, different light, different sounds, different stone — but it is not magical. It is geology and distance made visible.
- **R5.** Fossils and stones tell true stories about the past, but they do not explain the present. Captain Crunch proves ancient seas existed; it does not answer what is on the other side of the hill today.
- **R6.** A ten-year-old's body has real limits: how far he can walk before fatigue clouds judgment, how much he can carry, how long he can see and think clearly before tiredness makes him less careful.
- **R7.** The stars seen from the cardboard observatory on the near side are the same stars found from the far side, but the view is different. Distance changes what the same thing means.

## Texture

The world is solid and immediate: the weight of stone in a pocket, the sound of bicycle tires on gravel, the exact temperature of shade and sun. Nothing is ominous or waiting. Danger is simple and real — the getting-dark, the distance walked, the tired batteries, the dog's hesitation, the unfamiliar path. Wonder is not hidden or magical. It is what you find when you actually look: the layers in a rock, the geometry of the hill, the infinite sameness and sameness-with-difference of stars. Everything is close enough to touch, far enough to take effort, strange enough to be interesting.
