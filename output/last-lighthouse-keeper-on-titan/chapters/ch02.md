# Chapter 2 — Dead Node, Live Signature

The registration record came up off the local store, no uplink needed: 2131, her thumbprint, her voiceprint sampled in a warm room at Station 7, and beneath it the custody signature for survey pod Wick-3. Permanent and non-transferable. Marit Anselm read the clause twice, the way she read fault codes, looking for the interpretation that let her stop.

There wasn't one. The beacon in Wick-3 had been speaking in her voice since before it sank, because the Authority had decided once that a rescuer should hear a person and not a tone. The registered wearer that winter was Halvard Ness. The words were his. He had composed them while the pod went under on a re-anchoring run she had ordered.

She walked the custody stamps backwards, hop by hop, and found the place where the chain stopped being a chain. Buoy 47, 2138, custody accepted. After that, nothing — no handoff, no acknowledgement, only the buoy's own re-transmit stamps, a thirty-day cache refusing to let go because nothing had ever told it the message was delivered. Its uplink horn was tholin-fouled. It could hear. It could not be heard. For nine years it had rebroadcast into an empty arc, out of every line of sight on Kraken Mare, re-signing its own custody each cycle like a man repeating a sentence to keep from forgetting it.

She overlaid the buoy's drift on the *Sorrel Draught*'s real track, not the filed one. Oyuna Brecht's anchor had fouled the derelict eight days ago and dragged it roughly fourteen kilometres east — inside the seventeen-kilometre horizon of a sixty-metre mast. The network had been repaired by accident, by a crime.

Delphine Sarr sold her uplink seconds at the posted rate and not one kilobit of courtesy. Anselm spent them on the 2139 inquest evidence list. What came back, three passes later, was not testimony but a query filter: *node classes excluded — dead*. Nobody had read Buoy 47's log, because a buoy that has never handed off a bundle reports as dead.

On the open distress band, thin through the drizzle static, Tomas Weir's cooperative logged the same unanswered call and timed it. Seventy-two hours, and the beacon became abandoned property. She was not the only listener.

The last thing she did took an hour and killed the hope. The fresh stamps were genuine — new emissions, every forty minutes, from Wick-3's radioisotope beacon, still fed, still patient, one hundred eighty metres down in five hundred kilograms per cubic metre of methane-ethane.

A failed seal at ninety-four kelvin kills by conduction in under ninety seconds. The pod's tank had gone in 2138.

Halvard Ness died in 2138. Nothing down there was alive. The machine had simply kept its promise for nine years, and she had been nine years out of earshot.
