# Mysteries — Bittern Station

Each is a promise the outline must pay. All four are answerable inside the Rules: no process aboard makes water without spending oxygen or hydrogen already on inventory, so every answer here is an accounting answer — mass that was always present and never counted, mass crossing a boundary assumed sealed, or mass logged without ever being measured.

- **Where do the +2.3 kg/day come from, and what number proves it?** The answer is a number and Brede's instrument is the only thing that can state it. Nothing is being created: the 300 m heated slurry line drives bound water out of the hydrated carbonates of the new lens Okoye cut into on Day −96, and that vapour crosses into the loop downstream of an inlet totalizer that counts liquid flow only. It was always in the seam; it was never product, so it was never counted. The proof is the D/H ratio of loop water shifted off the crew-loop signature toward the Vinalia seam signature by the proportion that exactly resolves 2.3 kg of seam-origin water in 61 kg of daily throughput — a shift of the order of tens of ppm, inside a ±10 ppm instrument, and therefore only real if Brede takes enough samples to beat her own error bar. The mine is not a mystery to solve so much as an assay never rerun.
- **Why did Stack B die forty days ago, twenty-nine days before the surplus appeared?** The failure came first and looks unrelated, and it is not. Whatever crosses the boundary carries carbonate with it; the membranes were fouled, not aged, and Vance has never opened the bag he sealed on Day −40 to see which. The question the book must answer is whether the same mechanism that is handing them free water is what took a third of their oxygen away, and therefore whether fixing one kills the other — because Stack A is downstream of the same loop.
- **Who told Kestrel to load a replacement stack before anyone aboard asked for one?** The tug launched carrying exactly the part Bittern needs and is sixty-one days out, arriving with twenty-nine days of air left in the dewars and registry law waiting for a distress key that has not been pressed. Nobody aboard requested it. The answer is in the schema: the automatic Cold Harbor pull on Day −29 carried the fault flag out of the station without a human transmitting it, and the operator that owns the logbook schema also owns who saw it next. The question is not whether Bittern was betrayed but whether a form can betray you, and whose name is at the bottom of it.
- **Twenty-five kilograms should be visible, and the tanks read nominal — where did it go?** Ultrasonic level is ±1.5 %, ±9 kg on a 600 kg tank; 25.3 kg accumulated over eleven days is well outside that and should be standing in plain sight. It is not, because the 612 kg loop inventory figure has never been an instrument reading — it is a number carried forward in the Cold Harbor entry schema, and Marchetti has been rounding each day's entry back to nominal in the honest belief that a discrepancy inside daily tolerance is not yet a fact. The water is real, aboard and in the loop. The thing that vanished is the record of it, and the cost of that is the five days the watch did not have.
