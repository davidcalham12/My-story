# Chapter 2 — Inside Tolerance

The printer in the command module was older than anyone aboard and it printed the way it always had, four lines a second, so that eleven days of Cold Harbor schema came off the roller slowly enough for Marchetti to read them as they arrived. He did not want to read them as they arrived. He stood with his hands flat on the bench and let the paper feed over the edge and into the catch tray, and the sweetish carbonate smell came off the warm rollers with the paper, the way it came off everything.

Eleven entries. Day −11 through Day −1, and then the two Pike had opened the finding across, which made thirteen sheets in the tray and eleven of them his.

He lifted them out and squared the edges against the bench and went down the right-hand margin with a finger, because the signature block on the Cold Harbor schema sits low and right, under the environmental fields, where a man does not look at it unless he is looking for it.

G. MARCHETTI, RECYCLER TECH. Eleven times. Under each one, in field 14, LOOP INVENTORY (KG): 612.

He had known that. He had known it the way you know the pitch of a pump — as a condition of the room and not a fact about it. Seeing it stacked was different. Field 14 was mandatory. He had built a career of small exactitudes on the understanding that mandatory meant the form required a number, and that supplying a required number was not the same act as asserting one. He put his thumb on the field and tried, on the screen, to leave it blank. The schema returned the entry with the field boxed in red and the word REQUIRED. He tried the flag character the Consortium used for provisional values, the caret, and the schema stripped it and returned 612 and would not accept the entry until he acknowledged that it had. There was no comment field attached to field 14. There was a comment field attached to field 22, water quality, and one attached to field 31, consumables, and the schema did not let a comment cross a field boundary.

So the form permitted exactly one statement about the loop inventory, and it was 612, and it was mandatory, and he had made it eleven times over his own name.

He went back for the origin of it, because that was the disciplined thing and because he wanted very much for it to have an origin.

The handover entry, Day −300. Pike's signature as officer of the watch, his own as incoming custodian. Field 14: 612, and beside it in the provenance column the notation CF — carried forward. He opened the prior watch's final entry. Field 14: 612, CF. The watch before that, 612, CF. He went back through fourteen months, and then another fourteen, and then he stopped opening entries one at a time and queried the field across the whole station record, and the schema gave him a column of 612 that scrolled for a long time without changing a digit, all the way down to commissioning, where field 14 read 612 and the provenance column read DESIGN.

Not measured. Designed. The wet volume of the loop as drawn, before anyone filled it.

He searched the maintenance record for an ultrasonic reading of the potable loop and found none. There was no station history of anyone putting a transducer on it. There was no fitting to put one on. Level was read on the tanks at ±1.5%, which on a 600 kg tank was ±9 kg and useless for this, and the loop itself was a sealed circuit of pipe and membrane stack and surge bottle that nothing aboard had ever looked into. The number the whole watch had reasoned from — the number Pike had subtracted 25.3 from in her head at the brief, the number he had defended — had never been produced by an instrument. It was a drawing. It was sixty years of men copying a drawing and signing it.

He sat down with that for a while. The pumps went on doing what they did.

Then he did the other thing, the one he had been putting off, which was to reconstruct his own rounding.

He laid the totalizer strips beside the schema entries and worked them day by day. Day −11: inlet 61.0, outlet 63.3, difference +2.3 on 61 kg throughput, which was +3.8% against a ±0.5% daily tolerance. He had entered the day inside tolerance. His reasoning was written in his own margin annotation in his own hand: *TOT ±0.02/pass, mult. passes, within instr.* The instruments counted to ±0.02 kg per pass and the loop made many passes a day, and he had argued to himself, defensibly, that a stack of small uncertainties could grow to cover 2.3 kg, and that a discrepancy inside instrument tolerance was not yet a fact, and that a man does not put not-yet-facts into a Consortium record.

Day −10, +2.3. Day −9, +2.3. He wrote them down the margin of a blank strip in a column because he wanted to see it the way Pike had made him see it, as a column and not as a series of days each of which had been reasonable.

Eleven days. 2.3, eleven times, was 25.3 kg.

And in field 14, eleven times, he had entered the change as nothing at all. Not wrongly. That was the part he kept returning to and could not get past: every single entry was defensible on its own, and the form had no way to hold the sum, and so the sum had gone into the only place available to it, which was nowhere.

He was still sitting there when he opened the transmission log, out of the same mechanical thoroughness, to see what the Consortium had been sent while all this was not being recorded.

The log was mostly nothing. Scheduled schema pulls, one a week, a few hundred kilobytes each, no human hand on them. He scrolled back past Day −11, past Day −29 —

And stopped.

0412, Day −29. AUTO SCHEMA PULL — CH. Not a scheduled one. An unscheduled pull, triggered on content, and the content line listed the fields it had taken, and among them was field 09, EQUIPMENT FAULT, carrying the stack-fault flag. Marchetti knew that flag intimately. He had set it himself on Day −40, in this chair, the evening Vance bagged Stack B, because the schema required a fault flag on a failed unit and he was loyal to the form.

Sent by no one. Acknowledged by Cold Harbor thirty-one minutes later, which was simply how far away they were. And within the day, on a manifest he had no business having seen and had seen anyway, on the Kestrel loading list: one electrolysis stack, membrane type, Bittern spec.

Eighteen days before the loop began coming back rich, a machine at Bittern had told a machine at Cold Harbor something no one aboard had decided to say. Twenty-nine days before Pike put her strip on the table.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed the transmission log without printing it.

He took the 612 to Pike in the galley and put the commissioning entry in front of her with the provenance column showing, and said that the number was a drawing and had never been measured and that he had signed it eleven times. She read it twice and said, "All right. That's a finding." She did not ask what else he had opened. He did not offer it.

Vance came through with the morning count on a card in his fist, 176.0 kg, and said it to the room the way he said it every morning: four kilos off the one-eighty in two days at two a day, eighty-eight days at the same draw, and the draw had not changed.

Marchetti went back to the command module and opened the Day +2 entry to sign it.

Field 14 was already filled in. 612. Provenance: CF.
