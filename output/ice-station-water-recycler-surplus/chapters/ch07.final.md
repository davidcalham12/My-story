# Chapter 7 — Thirty-Three

The sheet was the back of a totalizer strip, cut square and turned over. Pike ruled it down the middle with the edge of the pencil and wrote **KEY** on the left and **HOLD** on the right, and then sat for a while with the pumps going and did not write anything else, because the two columns were not the hard part. The hard part was that they were both arithmetic and only one of them could be undone, and it was not the one she wanted.

Left column first.

*Key at 2300. CH has it 2331. Early burn authorised or not authorised inside twenty-four hours — assume authorised, Kestrel wants it. Arrival Day +47.* Under that: *claim open at 2331. First hull alongside. Kestrel is the only hull.*

Right column: *Do not key. Kestrel Day +61 on present trajectory. No burn available after today — the window closes with the phasing.*

Then across the bottom, under both, the line that made the sheet worth ruling at all. She wrote it out longhand because she wanted to see the words rather than the digits.

*Vance: Stack A cell voltage 1.82 V Day −40, +4 mV/day, straight. Stack B quit at 2.10 V. Stack A reaches 2.10 on Day +30.* Day +30 was three days ago. *After that Stack A makes nothing and the draw goes 2.0 to 5.1.*

And the standing number, which she did in the margin in her own hand the way she had done every morning since Day 0, not because she doubted Vance's column but because a number you have not subtracted yourself is a number you are quoting.

180.0 start. Thirty-three days at 2.0 the day, the standing shortfall, the term that ate everything: 66.0. That left 114.0, and 114.0 was where the arithmetic stopped being a subtraction and started being a history.

Less 0.4, Okoye's two cycles on Day +6, the walk that found the tie-in.
Less 0.78, Brede's six hours with Stack A down on Day +11.
Less 3.1, the full day down on Day +24, the day they opened the bag.
Less 0.4, the two cycles Okoye was going to spend this morning, which she had authorised at 0615 and which she entered now rather than later because she did not keep two sets of figures.

109.3 kg.

At 2.0 kg/day: 54.6 days. Day +33 plus 54.6 was Day +87, and she wrote *+87* and boxed it, and looked at the box, and saw what she had been seeing for a week. The four subtractions below the line came to 4.68 kg between them. The standing draw came to 66.0. Every argument aboard this station had been about the 4.68.

Day +87 was only true while the draw stayed 2.0.

Marchetti knocked on the frame at 0910 and did not come in, which was how he knocked.

He had one sheet too. He put it on the table face down, turned it over, and stepped back before she could read it, and that was the first thing that told her what it was.

The transmission log. An unscheduled automatic pull, Cold Harbor origin, 0412 on Day −29, and in the payload the stack-fault flag with the schema field beside it filled in and initialled *G.M.*

"No human sent that," he said. "The pull is automatic. But the field is mine. I filled it Day −40 because the schema requires it filled inside seventy-two hours of a plant fault, and the pull took it out at 0412 on the twenty-ninth, and Kestrel loaded a stack inside the day. I have had this since Day +2. I gave you the six-twelve and I kept this." He put his hands behind him. "I'd like to stand down from the log. Salas can keep the schema. I'll show her the entry rules this afternoon."

"No," Pike said.

He waited.

"You filled a field you were required to fill. If you'd left it blank the schema would have flagged you on Day −37 and someone else would have filled it on Day −36." She turned her own sheet over so he could see the box and the figure in it. "And the same schema is why six-twelve stood for ten years without an instrument on it. It carried the number forward because carrying forward is what the form does, and it carried the fault out because carrying out is what the form does. It did both. Your name is at the bottom of both." She set the pencil down. "You don't get to stand down from one of them. You'd be standing down from the wrong one."

"I rounded eleven entries."

"You did. That cost us five days and it is in the log in your hand and mine, and you will keep making the entries, because the next person to make them will make them worse and I will not be able to tell." She looked up. "Stand down when it stops mattering who fills the field. It hasn't stopped."

Okoye went out at 1055 and was at the valve at 1140. He called it through on the suit loop, flat, the way he called everything: isolation valve, hand wheel, thirty-one turns, hard stop, confirmed by feel through the glove. Then the plate — fifteen millimetre, jacketed, cut that morning on the bench, blanked at the flange downstream of the inlet totalizer. Four bolts. He read the torque out twice.

It stopped the vapour into the loop. It also stopped the slurry, and therefore the boiler, and therefore the billets, and therefore the contract tonnage, and therefore the +2.3 kg/day that was the only generous number on the station. One hand wheel and four bolts had closed all of it in the same motion, and Pike wrote *1140 isol. valve closed, tie-in blanked, S. Okoye* and beneath it *0.4 kg, two cycles. Eight remaining.*

Cold Harbor answered the previous day's query at 1622, sixty-two minutes late against the thirty-one each way. *Continue working the face at present rate. Book surplus as yield. No fault condition indicated.*

She logged it verbatim, every word, including *no fault condition indicated.* Then she logged beneath it that the officer of the watch had disregarded the instruction, that the face had been isolated at 1140, that the surplus was bound water from an uncounted carbonate lens crossing a liquid-only totalizer, and that the same vapour had scaled Stack B's membranes to failure and was forty days into Stack A's. She signed the instruction. Then she signed the disregard, separately, with the time on each.

At 2300 the command module was hers. The key was live. The declaration sat composed on the pad, eleven words, and thirty-one minutes on the far side of them.

She read her sheet again. Day +47 was inside the line. Day +61 was eight days outside it. Day +87 was only true while the draw stayed 2.0, and the draw stayed 2.0 only while Stack A held, and Stack A had passed 2.10 volts three days ago and had not quit — which meant either Vance's line was straight and Stack A was living on borrowed slope, or the line had bent when the vapour stopped.

Three days would tell her which. Three days cost 6.0 kg and the early burn.

She did not key it. She wrote instead: *Watch held to a Stack A cell voltage re-read, 0600 Day +36, T. Vance and N. Pike present. Distress not keyed this date. Early-burn window forgone knowingly.* She signed it.

Then she took the eleven strips out of her pocket, the ones that had never matched the logged ones, flattened them on the console, and entered all eleven into the permanent record — Day −11 through Day 0, each with its date, each marked BACKDATED, each initialled *N.P.* It took forty minutes. It was the same offence she had been holding against Marchetti since Day 0, and she entered it in her own hand, and signed that too.
