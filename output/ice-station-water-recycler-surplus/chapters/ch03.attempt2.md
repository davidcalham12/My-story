# Chapter 3 — A Valve You Can Feel

Salas tended the lock, because tending the lock was what Salas did when he was not on the 0200 watch, and because Okoye did not want Vance doing it. Vance would have stood at the panel with the consumable board up and read the cost of the trip out loud twice, once before and once after, and Okoye had already done that arithmetic on paper at 0530 and did not need it recited.

One eighty had been the number on Day 0. Six days at two a day was twelve, which put the dewars at 168.0 kg before he so much as touched the inner hatch. Two cycles to get out and back, 0.9 kg of atmosphere apiece, 1.8 kg total, of which 0.4 kg was oxygen. That left 167.6. Before the trip, 168.0 at the unchanged 2.0 kg/day draw had been 84.0 days still to run, and the ninety on the command module strip was counted from Day 0, not from this morning: six days spent plus eighty-four to come was ninety on the nose, true to the tenth and with nothing in hand. Spend 0.4 kg of oxygen and the remainder came back 83.8 — two tenths of a day, no more than that, but the ninety had no two tenths to give. Six plus 83.8 was 89.8, and 89.8 written in whole days does not read ninety, which meant the strip on the command module bulkhead was wrong from the moment he cracked the seal and would have to be re-cut.

He wrote 167.6 on the back of his glove checklist in pencil so he would not have to take anyone's word for it later.

Salas ran the fit check. Okoye did the last of it himself anyway — wrists, neck ring, the left knee seal that had been weeping argon-dry talc for a month — because a fit check you watch on a display is a report and a fit check you do with your thumbs is a fit check. The pumps changed pitch as the lock drew down, that long descending complaint, and then there was no pitch at all, only the suit, and 0.028 g, and the dark.

Ceres does not make a landscape out of its dark. It makes a floor. He went up the cut at a walking pace that would have been a fall anywhere else, the regolith taking his boot and giving it back with nothing like conviction, and the slurry line ran beside him the whole way, jacketed, lagged, strapped at two-metre intervals to a rail he had bolted himself in his first month.

Three hundred metres. He put a glove on every jacket section.

That was the point of coming. The line had forty-one heater zones and every one of them reported a temperature to a screen inboard, and Okoye had never once signed a heater figure off that screen, because a thermocouple tells you what the thermocouple is doing and a glove tells you what the line is doing. He worked up the run section by section, palm flat, counting three seconds, feeling the warmth come through the layup as a slow pressure rather than a heat. Section eleven. Section twelve. The jacket at zone nineteen was hotter than its neighbours and he stopped there and knew exactly why before he checked the local dial: Day −14, a slug in the line, and he had raised the zone setpoint fourteen degrees to drive it through and clear the head.

The dial said fourteen degrees high. He had left it there. Twenty days.

He had logged it as a slug. That was all he had logged — *slug cleared, zone 19, line normal* — because at the time there had been nothing else true to write. A setpoint is not a finding. A setpoint is a thing you do to a line to make it behave, and he had done it, and then a stack had failed forty-six days back and a loop had started coming back rich seventeen days back and nobody had asked him what temperature the line was running at, because nobody had thought the line was in the question.

He stood with his glove on zone nineteen for longer than three seconds.

Then he went on to the isolation valve, because that was the other thing he had come for and the only thing he would sign today.

The log said full open. The log had said full open for three hundred days. Okoye got both gloves on the stem and felt the collar, and the stem was out — out to the shoulder, out to the stop, the threads bare and cold and gritty with fines the way an open stem is. He worked the handwheel a quarter turn shut and a quarter turn back and felt the packing drag and then release. Full open. Confirmed the only way it can be confirmed, which is with a hand on it. He would sign that.

It was turning away from the valve body that he found the other thing.

Downstream of the inlet totalizer — downstream, on the loop side, past the point where anything entering the potable system is supposed to have been counted — there was a tie-in. Fifteen millimetres, maybe. A short jacketed stub off the crown of the line, running up and aft in a lazy arc and then in through the gland plate toward the recycler house, toward the vapour-compression stage. It was strapped, it was lagged, it was neat. Somebody had built it on purpose and built it well.

It was not on any drawing aboard Bittern. He had all of them memorised, the as-builts and the commissioning set and the two revision sheets, and there was no line on any of them off the crown of the slurry line between the totalizer and the gland plate. There had never been.

He put his glove on it.

Warm. Not line-warm, not zone-warm — a thinner heat, the heat of something moving fast through a small bore, and the stub carried a faint high tremble into his palm that pipe does not do when it is carrying liquid. Liquid shoves. Liquid thumps and surges. This whispered. He held it for ten seconds by the suit clock, then moved his hand forty centimetres along and held it again to be sure it was not sun and not a heater strap, and there was no sun here and no strap there, and it was still warm.

He did not say anything on the loop. Salas was on the loop.

He took his samples last, because samples are the part you do when your hands are already tired and you want them done right. Two chips. One off the fresh face, from the lens he had advanced the head into on Day −96, prying with the flat of the chisel until a palm-sized wafer came away in that slow falling way that nothing on Ceres ever finishes. One off the worked-face product where it sat on the billet rail, hard and clean and old.

He bagged them, sealed them, and wrote on the labels with the grease pencil: FACE, LENS, D−96 ADVANCE, DAY +6. And: BILLET RAIL, WORKED FACE PRODUCT, DAY +6.

Location and date. Nothing else. He thought he knew what the first one was and he was not going to write his thinking on a label that was going to end up in Brede's hand, because Brede would read it, and Brede would then be measuring a sample that had already been told what it was. Let the vials say it or let them not.

Two cycles spent. Ten left in the budget.

He came inboard damp, still in the undersuit, hair flat, the carbonate sweetness on him worse than usual, and he went to the watch desk with the two bags in one hand and the pencilled checklist in the other before he had even racked the hard shell.

"Full open," he said. "Stem out. I felt it. You can log that."

Pike waited.

"There's a tie-in," Okoye said, "off the crown of the line, downstream of the inlet totalizer. Fifteen mil. Goes into the vapour-compression stage. It is not on any drawing on this station and it is warm and it is not carrying liquid — you can feel that it isn't."

"Meaning."

"Meaning the inlet totalizer counts litres." He set the bags on the desk, squarely, side by side. "It's a liquid flow meter. It counts liquid going past it at plus or minus two hundredths of a kilo a pass, and it does that honestly, and it has been doing it honestly for three hundred days. That stub is carrying steam." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "Nothing aboard Bittern has ever counted steam as anything at all."

Somewhere aft a pump changed pitch, and four people heard it, and nobody looked up.

"And 167.6," he said. "Re-cut the strip. It's eighty-nine."
