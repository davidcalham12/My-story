# Chapter 6 — The Bag

The last pair went into the inlet at 0540, and Brede logged the time before she logged the number, because the time was the only thing she was certain of yet.

Thirteen days. Day +11 through Day +24, one loop sample every morning at the same point in the same watch, paired with an outlet sample on the days she could afford one. She had not been able to afford all of them. Nineteen vials on Day +11 — twenty-two at the start of all this, less two spent on Okoye's chips from the worked face, less one on the first loop run that told her nothing she could sign — and the series had eaten fifteen. Four left in the rack, standing in their foam like teeth in a jaw. On five of the thirteen days she had skipped the outlet sample and written *outlet omitted, vial ration* in the margin of the run sheet in her own hand, because the alternative was a series that looked complete and was not, and she would rather hand Pike a gapped series than a tidy lie.

She ran the regression on paper first. She always did. The screen would give her the same answer in a quarter second and she would not believe it until she had seen the residuals herself, spread across thirteen rows, none of them large, none of them leaning.

Slope: 2.38 ppm per day. Standard error on the slope: 0.30.

She sat with that for a moment in the warm noise of the med bay, the condensate running its slow bead down the cold face of the bulkhead behind the spectrometer, and did the division. Two point three eight over point three zero. Seven point nine.

"Seven point nine," she said aloud, to the empty room, and then, because it would be read by people who needed a word and not a decimal, "eight. Call it eight."

She wrote the sentence out once, crossed it out for a hedge she did not like, and wrote it again.

*Loop D/H is increasing at 2.38 ± 0.30 ppm per day over thirteen days, non-zero at approximately eight standard errors. This is a slope. It is not, by itself, a cause.*

She signed it. The word *cause* was absent on purpose and she wanted anyone reading to feel its absence like a missing tooth. A slope was a correlation with a direction. What made it a mechanism was somebody else's evidence, and she did not have it yet.

Then she turned the arithmetic inside out, which was the part she had been waiting thirteen days to do.

Pike came at 0700 and stood where she always stood, one hand on the frame, not touching the bench.

"Say it small," Pike said.

"Small first, then the useful one." Brede turned the sheet round. "If 2.3 kilograms a day of water at 820 ppm is entering a loop holding L kilograms at 171, the loop's ratio climbs by 2.3 times 649, divided by L, every day. That's forced. The only unknown on the right is L."

"And you've got the left."

"I've measured the left. 2.38." She wrote it. "So L equals 2.3 times 649 over 2.38. Fourteen ninety-two point seven over two point three eight. Six hundred and twenty-seven kilograms."

Pike did not move. "Error bar."

"Twelve point six percent on the slope carries straight through. Twelve point six percent of 627 is seventy-nine. Six hundred and twenty-seven, plus or minus eighty."

"Six-twelve," Pike said.

"Six-twelve is inside it. So is six-twelve plus the twenty-five point three Marchetti rounded away — that's six three seven point three, and it sits closer to my centre than the design figure does." Brede put the pencil down. "Nora. Six-twelve has never been measured. Not at commissioning, not since, not by anything. There's no fitting to measure it with. This is the first instrument estimate of the loop inventory anyone aboard Bittern has ever made, and it is an estimate, and it has an eighty-kilogram bar on it. But the water Gil has been rounding out of the log is in the loop. It's been in the loop the whole time. What went missing was the record."

Pike looked at the sheet for longer than she needed to. "That's a slope," she said. "Not a cause."

"That's what I signed."

Vance brought the cause at 0930, and he brought it in a bag.

He carried it through from the workshop held out from his body the way you carry something that might still be alive, and set it on the med bay bench: Stack B, sealed and labelled in his own block capitals on Day −40, forty days of not opening it, and now twenty-four more. The seal came apart with a crack that Brede felt in her teeth.

"I expected grey," he said.

They were not grey. Membranes that had died of age came out grey and thinned and soft at the folds, and these came out white — crusted, matte, a bloom across the active face like frost that had been dried onto it and then dried again.

Brede swabbed two square centimetres and ran it.

"Sodium carbonate," she said. "Not wear. Scale."

Vance sat down. It was the only time she had seen him do that.

He took Stack A off the line at 1100 for its own feed-side swab, and before he pulled the breaker he wrote the cost on the strip, the way he always did, the figures small and upright: *full day, 3.1 kg. 180.0 less 0.4 less 0.78 less 3.1 = 127.7. At 2.0/day, 63.8 days. Day +87.* He drew a line through *eighty-nine* on the board and wrote *eighty-seven* above it, and he wrote it before he opened the breaker and not after.

Stack A's membranes came out white too. Thinner scale, a lighter bloom, the same crystal under the loupe — forty days behind and going the same way at the same rate.

Pike came back at 1600 and asked for the one-line version.

Brede gave it with the bars in it, because there was no version of it that was true without them.

"The fresh lens has been handing us two things down the same tie-in. Scale since Okoye cut the new face on Day −96, and water since he raised zone nineteen fourteen degrees on Day −14. The scale is on Stack B and it is starting on Stack A. The water is 2.3 kilograms a day into a loop I now measure at 627 ± 80. Same pipe. Same vapour. One source." She waited. "Close the isolation valve and the scale stops. Close the isolation valve and the water stops. It is one motion. There is no valve position that gives us one and not the other."

Pike said, "And the water is what we've been drinking."

"The water is what we've been living on," Brede said. "Since Day −11. Sixty-one kilos a day through the loop and two point three of it free, and the free two point three is the thing that is killing the stacks."

The pumps went on making the noise they had made all day. Nobody had put that water there. That was the whole finding, and the price of it was the water.
