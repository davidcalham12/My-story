# Chapter 5 — Cell Voltage, Morning

At 0527 Vance was already in the LOX bay with the ullage sheet on the clipboard and a pen he had carried since before the rotation, because the pens aboard leaked in the cold and this one did not. He did not turn on the summary screen. That was the whole of his method and he had never once explained it to anyone, because explaining it would have made it sound like superstition instead of what it was, which was the refusal to let a number tell him what to see before he had seen it.

Dewar one. He put his hand flat on the outer jacket first, the way he had since Day −40, feeling for the frost band and finding it where it had been yesterday and a finger's width lower. Ullage pressure, 118 kPa. Level by the ultrasonic, and he wrote the raw figure and then the ±1.5 % beside it in the margin because the number without its error was a rumour. Dewar two, warmer side, frost band lower again. Pressure 117. He wrote both.

Then the arithmetic, in pen, on paper, the same seventeen lines he had written seventeen mornings running.

One hundred eighty point zero at Day 0. Two point zero a day, because six people breathe 5.1 and Stack A at sixty per cent derate makes 3.1 and the difference comes out of these two bottles and nowhere else. Seventeen days is thirty-four. That is 146.0. Less the two airlock cycles Okoye spent walking the line on Day +6, which vented 0.9 kg of atmosphere each and 0.2 kg of that oxygen, so 0.4. That is 145.6. Less the 0.78 Stack A did not make in the six hours Vance had broken it so Brede could swab the feed side, which he had signed for and would sign for again.

144.8.

He looked at it a moment. Then he woke the screen, and the screen said 144.8, and he felt the small clean satisfaction of a man whose paper had been right and who would have believed the paper anyway.

Seventy-two point four days at the present draw. Day +17 and seventy-two point four is Day +89. He wrote *89* and circled it, which he had done every morning for seventeen mornings, and the circle had moved by exactly nothing, which was the only good news the station had.

It was 0554. The 0600 watch would not want the bay. Vance sat down on the toolbox with the clipboard on his knee and, for no reason he could name afterwards except that the number 89 had been obedient and he had a spare six minutes, pulled Stack A's cell-voltage history.

He had not looked at it since Day −40. That was the point of a derate: you set it, the stack runs inside it, and you stop looking, because looking at a thing that is behaving is how you talk yourself into running it harder. He pulled forty days because forty was the habit-number, and then extended it because the file went back further, and then he stopped moving.

1.82 volts on Day −40. 1.824. 1.828. 1.832.

He counted along it with the pen tip and did not write anything for a while. Four millivolts a day. Not a scatter, not a step, not the ragged thing a fouled cell does when it is dying in patches. A line. Fifty-seven points with a rule laid along them.

Cell voltage climbs when the membrane resists. The stack is drawing the same current and paying more for it, and a derate does not do that; a derate is a ceiling, and this was a floor rising to meet one. He wrote: *1.82 + 57 × 0.004*. He did the multiplication longhand because it was 0559 and he wanted to be wrong. 0.228. 2.048.

He went to the panel and read live cell voltage off the instrument itself, hand on the housing, the way he read everything.

2.05.

Vance sat back down. Stack B's record was in the same archive, forty days stale and never touched since he had bagged the brick and written the label. He would not open the bag. He had told Pike that on Day 0 and he meant it this morning more than he had then. But the file was not the bag. He scrolled Stack B to its last hour and read the number it had been holding when it quit taking current.

2.10 volts.

From 1.82 at four millivolts a day, 2.10 is seventy days. Day −40 plus seventy.

Day +30.

He wrote it and then wrote the rest of it, because the rest of it was only subtraction and he had never in his life stopped a subtraction halfway. Thirteen more days from this morning at 2.0 a day is 26.0 off 144.8. One hundred eighteen point eight kilograms standing on Day +30. And on Day +30 Stack A makes nothing at all, so the draw is not 2.0, it is the whole 5.1, six people at 0.85 each with nothing coming in.

118.8 over 5.1. He did it twice. Twenty-three point three days.

Day +30 plus twenty-three is Day +53.

Kestrel arrives Day +61.

He sat with the clipboard and the two circles on it, the 89 he had drawn at 0554 and the 53 he had drawn at 0603, and understood that the 89 had never been a number about oxygen. It had been a number about Stack A holding, and Stack A had been telling him for fifty-seven mornings, in four-millivolt increments, exactly when it would stop.

Eight days. On the wrong side.

Kestrel called at 1140, nineteen minutes of light-lag each way now, which made it less a conversation than a pair of letters read aloud. The voice was unhurried and pleasant and used the watch by name: hoped Pike was getting sleep, asked whether Brede had vials enough, said the replacement stack was in the hold, bagged, seals intact, and that they had checked it that morning against the manifest. Then, at the end, in the same register as the rest: *And we can burn early the instant Bittern declares. Say the word and we shave days off. No pressure from us either way.*

Vance logged the traffic. He did not answer it; Pike answered traffic.

He did not go to her that night.

He thought about it through the 1800 and most of the 2200, and what stopped him was not fear and not doubt about the arithmetic. It was that he had been the one shouting on Day 0 to declare, and he had been overruled by a woman who would not sign a tank level, and she had been right not to sign it. A slope through fifty-seven points was not five days of accumulation. It was a projection. If he carried it to Pike at 2300 with his voice still up from Kestrel's call, it would arrive as the same argument he had already lost, wearing better clothes.

So at 2340 he tapped it through the bulkhead instead, in the old knock-code the two of them had fallen into weeks ago because Marchetti's pump ran loud and neither of them slept. Numbers went slowly. *2.05. 2.10. Day 30.*

A long pause. Then, from the other side, one word tapped out patiently, letter by letter: *tolerance*.

Vance put his knuckle against the cold plate and tapped back: *slope*.

Nothing after that. They lay in adjacent compartments with forty-one metres of regolith overhead, listening to each other's pumps hold their pitch, until Salas came through at 0200 for the relief and the sound of the hatch made them both, separately, pretend to be asleep.
