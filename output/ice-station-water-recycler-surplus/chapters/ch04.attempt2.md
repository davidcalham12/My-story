# Chapter 4 — Without a Baseline

The medical bay ran four degrees colder than the rest of Bittern because the spectrometer wanted it that way, and Hannah Brede had long ago stopped bringing a jacket. Cold hands were steadier. She had the two chips Okoye had brought her laid out on the anodized tray, each in its own foil sleeve, each labelled in his blunt hand with a location and a date and nothing else. No guess. No note saying *I think this is the one*. She had asked him for that on Day +6 and he had looked at her as though she had asked him not to breathe, and then he had done it.

She burned the worked face first.

Five millilitres, degassed, reduced over hot chromium, into the source. The instrument took eleven minutes to settle and she used them to write the run number, the date, the sleeve label and the cell temperature into the bound logbook, because the spectrometer's own memory held ninety runs and then began to eat the oldest, and she did not intend to lose a number to a ring buffer.

Worked face: 171 ppm D/H, ±10.

She wrote it with the error bar attached, because a number without one was a rumour.

The second chip was from the new lens — the carbonate Okoye had cut into ninety-six days ago and logged as a face change. Bound water, driven out of the mineral itself under the torch. It took longer to prepare and she was careful with it, because there had been twenty-two vials in the drawer when all this began and she had just spent two.

820 ppm, ±10.

She sat back on the stool and looked at the two numbers for a while with the fans going and the condensate crawling down the cold face of the bulkhead behind her.

Six hundred and forty-nine ppm apart.

That was luck, and she knew exactly how much of it she had been handed. Two reservoirs in the same crater, cut from the same seam by the same head, and they were separated by a factor of nearly five. If the lens had come in at 200 ppm she would have had nothing — no instrument aboard could have told the two apart, and the whole question of where the water came from would have stayed a story people told each other in the wardroom. Instead the seam had handed her a tracer. She did not believe in providence. She wrote *649 ppm separation — tracer viable* in the margin and moved on.

Then she ran the potable loop.

213 ppm, ±10.

She looked at that one for a long time too, and then she capped the vial, logged the run, and did not tell anybody.

Vance found her before the 1800 handover, which she had expected, because Vance counted his LOX by hand every morning and a man who did that could not help wanting numbers from other people.

"You ran the loop."

"I ran a sample of the loop."

"And?"

"And I'm not reporting it."

He waited. He was good at that, which was rarer aboard than he knew.

"Two hundred and thirteen parts per million," she said, "plus or minus ten. And that figure means nothing, Theo, and I want you to understand *why* it means nothing before you carry it out of this compartment, because you will carry it out of this compartment."

"It's six hundred short of the lens."

"It's forty-two above the worked face. Against what?" She turned the logbook so he could see the empty column. "There is no archived loop sample aboard this station from before Day minus eleven. I've been through the medical stores, the assay locker and Marchetti's sample racks. Nothing. Not one 5 ml vial of loop water from a day when the loop behaved. So I have a number, and I do not have the thing that makes a number a measurement. I don't know what this loop read on Day minus twenty. It could have sat at 213 since commissioning. It could have sat at 160. An offset without a baseline is not a measurement. It's a number with a story attached, and the story is the part you'd be carrying."

Vance took that. He did not like it. The two were separate operations with him.

"Then get a baseline."

"There isn't one to get. It's gone. It went out the outlet totalizer three hundred days ago." She put the pen down. "So I stop measuring the offset and I measure the slope instead."

That was the part she had worked out at 0300 with the pumps changing pitch through the deck.

A rate of change needed no baseline. If the seam was pushing 2.3 kg/day of 820 ppm water into a 612 kg loop sitting near 171, the loop had to *climb* — 2.3 times the difference, over the inventory, a shade under two and a half ppm every day. Call it 2.44. Thirteen days of that was thirty-two ppm, and thirty-two ppm was three times her error bar and visible from the far end of the compartment. One sample against nothing was invisible. Thirteen paired samples regressed against day number was an experiment her instrument could actually win, and it was the only one.

She needed the feed side clean first. Carryover from the outlet header would blur the paired samples and there was no point in starting a thirteen-day series with a dirty swab.

Vance broke Stack A for six hours so she could do it.

He wrote the cost on the strip before he opened the breaker, not after — 3.1 kg/day, six hours, a quarter of a day, 0.78 kg — in his own hand, with the time and his initials, while the stack was still running. Then he pulled it. Brede watched him do it and understood that this was the thing Vance had instead of an error bar.

At the wardroom she gave them the accounting the way it stood. One hundred and eighty at Day 0, less two a day for eleven days was 158.0. Less the 0.4 kg of oxygen Okoye had vented on his two cycles: 157.6. Less 0.78 for the outage: 156.8 kg. At 2.0 kg/day that was 78.4 days. Day +11 plus 78.4 landed on Day +89.

Then she told them what she would not give them.

"Paired samples, loop and outlet, every day for thirteen days. I will have nothing before then. Not a trend, not a sense of it, not a feeling. I am asking the watch not to request a preliminary opinion in the interval, because if you ask me on day six I will either refuse you or I will guess, and the second one is worse."

Pike said, "Noted," and wrote it down, which was Pike's entire vocabulary for agreement.

Brede started the series that night with six vials.

Vance did the subtraction out loud, the way he did everything.

"Twenty-two. Two on Sam's chips. One on the loop. Six now." He looked up. "Thirteen left."
