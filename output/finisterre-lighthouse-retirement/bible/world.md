# World Bible: Finisterre Lighthouse Retirement

## Setting

Cabo Fisterra rises from the Atlantic as a granite fist, and the Finisterre lighthouse stands on its edge like a keeper who will not leave. The light-tower is white, narrow, and built to last; its lantern room commands the horizon from 147 meters above the sea. Below, the village of Finisterre clusters in the lee of the cape, its stone houses turning their backs to the wind, their gardens tucked behind stone walls. The sea here is the Costa da Morte—the Coast of Death—and the rocks know why.

Tomás's house sits between the village and the lighthouse path, close enough to hear the fog horn when the Atlantic rolls in thick and grey. Behind it, his tomato garden occupies a south-facing slope, sheltered by a greenhouse of old wood and plastic where the heat gathers like a secret. The soil there was amended with his own composted scraps for forty years. The village smell is salt, diesel from the fishing boats, and smoke from chimneys. The lighthouse smell is paraffin, paint, and the metal-sharp ozone of electricity jumping between contacts. The light itself rises through concrete and spiral iron, a climb of ninety-two steps to the gallery, then fourteen more to the lamp room. The granite beneath everything is older than records.

## Factions

- **The Lighthouse Service** — They want the light kept, the watch maintained, the equipment precise, and the logs written accurately. They regard Tomás as the thing they built the service around: a man who came when called, stayed when others left, and kept the lamp burning through the storm of 1987 when they could not reach him. In retirement, they release him with a pension and the knowledge that the automation they installed will never be tested the way he was.

- **The Village** — Fishermen, their families, the shopkeeper, the women who gather in doorways. They want the light burning and Tomás in the tower when the weather turns. They regard him as part of the cape itself, the quiet authority that does not change, does not leave, does not fail. His retirement unsettles them in ways they do not speak aloud.

- **The Mainland Administration** — They want automation, efficiency, a lighthouse run by electronics from an office hundreds of kilometers away. They regard Tomás as an expensive habit the service has outgrown, a man paid to stand in a tower that no longer needs standing.

- **The Household** — His wife, the Sunday table, the rhythms that repeat: the seasons of the garden, the timing of meals, the week that turns on its hinge every seven days. They want continuity and routine. They regard him as the keeper of all of it—not just the light, but the order that holds the days together.

## Craft and Equipment

- **The Rotating Optic** — A lens system of ground glass, rotating on a bed of mercury. The mercury keeps friction low; the rotation spreads the light in a pattern the maritime charts know by heart. It must be wound each hour to run the clockwork that turns it, a task that cannot be delegated.

- **The Standby Generator** — Diesel, loud, red, kept clean and fueled. It comes alive when the main supply fails, when the storm brings the pylon down or sea salt corrodes the connections. It is the backup that must never be needed and always be ready.

- **The Watch Log** — A leather-bound book where every four hours the keeper writes the weather, the light-state, the visibility, any ship sightings, any fault. Forty years of Tomás's handwriting marks the margins: neat, factual, unchanging.

- **The Paraffin Lamp and Backup Lights** — Older equipment stored in the equipment room: a paraffin era made small and kept. No one trusts entirely that the new will never fail.

- **The Tomato Greenhouse** — Wood frame, plastic panels, glass bottles of saved seeds labeled in brown ink with variety and year. The soil is organized by bed: early varieties nearest the south wall, late-keeping types where afternoon shade begins. The thermometer reads by the hour; the watering is done by hand.

- **The Sunday Table** — Where the family gathers, where the octopus-and-accountant joke is told at the same point every week, where bread is broken and Tomás speaks the same words he spoke last week and will speak next.

## Rules

- **R1.** The keeper on watch may not leave the lighthouse during his watch except for the equipment room check or movement between lamp room and gallery; no absence longer than ten minutes is permitted while the light burns.

- **R2.** The rotating optic must never be stopped while darkness is falling or while darkness remains; it turns from dusk through dawn, every night, without exception.

- **R3.** The watch log is written every four hours on the hour in ink, with the time, weather, and light-state recorded; no entry is false, no entry is omitted, and no entry is amended after it is written.

- **R4.** The Sunday joke about the octopus and the accountant is told only when the family is seated at the table with the meal ready, and it is told in the same words Tomás has told it every Sunday for decades.

- **R5.** The tomato plants are planted in March, staked through July, picked green or ripe depending on weather and season's length, and the greenhouse is emptied by November; no planting is done in winter months.

- **R6.** The garden soil receives the household compost from the kitchen and nothing bought or chemical; what grows there grows from what was already theirs.

- **R7.** The people in the house do not speak of the storm of 1987 to one another. This prohibition does not extend to the watch log, to what Tomás remembers alone in the tower, or to what is said outside the house.

- **R8.** The lighthouse interior holds objects that belong to the watch—the log, the key to the lamp room door, the paraffin backup lanterns, the compass, the barometer—and these do not leave the tower except that the outgoing keeper may pass them in person and by hand to a named successor or heir at the hour his watch formally ends.

## Texture

Granite, salt-wind, fog. The smell of slow things: bread rising, tomato leaves drying on the sill, diesel and paraffin and the old-metal smell of a winding key turned by the same hand for years. The sound is the fog horn in grey weather, the sea against the rocks below, the creak of wooden stairs in the light tower, the wind through plastic seams of the greenhouse. The taste is strong tea, dark bread, tomatoes with their own salt still on the skin. The light here is Atlantic light—honest, without sentiment, hard on the granite and kind to nothing. This is a world where small things matter: the consistency of the log entry, the repetition of the joke, the precise planting date, the mercury in its bath. It is a world earned, not given.
