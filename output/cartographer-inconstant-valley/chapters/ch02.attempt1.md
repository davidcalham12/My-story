# Chapter 2 — Ten Arrows

The morning came up grey and windless, and by seven the low pastures above Nether Scarrow were steaming where the sun found them. Hale had his station pegged before the dew was off: a stake driven at the corner of the walled ground where the lane met the intake, and beside it the tripod, and on the wall-top his field book in its oilskin.

Nat Fowler came down the lane with the ten arrows in a bundle under his arm and the Ingleford lad trailing behind him, a thin boy of perhaps sixteen named Coulter who had been hired at a shilling the day and had not yet stopped looking at the fell as though it might come nearer. Fowler said good morning. He said the weather would hold till noon and not past it. Then he took the leading end of the chain and went out along the line Hale had set him, and he did not say another word that was not needed.

The chain was Hale's own, a Gunter's of a hundred links, bought new in Lancaster the previous autumn on the same note as the theodolite, and he had tested it twice against a standard and found it honest to a quarter of a link. He liked the sound it made going out — the small iron conversation of the links, then the check, then the silence while Fowler set his arrow and Hale, at the rear, brought the handle up to the mark and read the ground.

They ran the line along the Sile, keeping the beck on their left, the water low and brown over its stones. Fowler was a good chainman. He did not drag and he did not snatch; he laid the chain where Hale's staff told him and he set each arrow upright and clean, pressing it down with the ball of his thumb, and then he walked on. Behind him Coulter drew each arrow as he came to it and passed it forward at the changes. It was the only honest thing Hale had met since he came over the ford, and he let himself enjoy it for the better part of an hour.

At the tenth arrow Fowler stopped and stood waiting.

"Count," Hale said.

Fowler came back along the line, gathering as he came, and Hale watched him do it and understood a moment before it happened that he was not going to hear a number. The chainman stopped an arm's length off. He took Hale's right hand by the wrist, without hurry and without discourtesy, turned it palm up, and laid the arrows into it one after another, iron on skin, until the bundle was all there and cold and his own hands were empty.

"That's what's in your hand, sir," Fowler said. "I'll not say it for you."

Hale stood with the arrows in his palm. Ten of them: he could feel it before he counted, the weight familiar as coin. He counted them anyway, aloud, because he wanted to hear the figures spoken in that field by somebody, and Fowler waited politely through it and looked at the beck.

"Ten chains," Hale said. "And I took it myself."

"Aye," said Fowler, which committed him to nothing at all.

He wrote the entry there, at the station, standing, with the book open on the wall-top and the ink-horn of iron-gall wedged in the stones: the bearing, the date, the ten chains and the fifty-three links of the short pull beyond, and the description of the mark. Wet ink in a north wind dries slow. He blew on it and turned to the wall.

Two farmers had been leaning on it since the second change, elbows on the coping, watching the chain go out the way men watch a hare. One was grey and one was not. They had been there long enough to have seen every arrow set.

"You'll have seen the line run," Hale said. "It wants two names. You stood here for the taking of it; that's all that's asked."

The grey one looked at the book for a while with real interest, the way a man looks at another man's dog.

"We've no call to be writing in your book," he said.

"It costs you nothing. It says only what you saw."

"Aye," said the other, agreeably, and neither of them moved, and neither of them left, and after a moment the grey one asked after the health of Hale's horse. They were not hostile. That was the thing Hale would set down that evening and then cross out. They stayed at the wall the whole while, and they watched him be refused, and they were sorry about it in a distant way, as one is sorry about weather.

Brock had been there since six. He had come down from Hollins Hall in the dark with a lantern he had not needed by the time he arrived, and he had stood out of the sight-line all morning without once being in the way, and he had a pen.

"You'll want a second hand on that, Mr. Hale, and I'm stood here already." He came over the grass, unstoppering his own ink. "I've watched it from the peg. Every arrow of it."

"You have."

"I have." Brock signed, a clear steady agent's hand. "And the lad — you're Ingleford, aren't you? Not dale?"

"Ingleford, sir," said Coulter.

"Then you'll write your name under mine, and mind you do it before Mr. Hale steps off this station, or it's worth nothing to anybody."

The boy wrote it, tongue out. Hale had not left the station. He noted that he had not, in the margin, in the same ink.

In the afternoon he walked the line again against the enclosure plan of 1836, unrolled on the wall and held with stones, and here the pleasure of the morning went out of the day. By the plan the distance from the lane corner to the elbow of the beck was rather more than eleven chains. He had it at ten and fifty-three. A surveyor's error, in rough ground, in a bad season, with a stretched chain — he could allow two links, three, five at the outside, and he was generous by nature with other men's arithmetic. He could not allow forty-seven. And where the plan put a field corner, a stone-built corner with an angle of some sixty degrees against the beck, there was no corner, no rubble, no line of different grass, nothing but pasture running unbroken down to the water as though nothing had ever been built there and nothing pulled down.

He stood a long while looking at the place where the corner was not.

At dusk he took the plan to the church, and found Amos Dent in the porch with the door on the latch behind him and the light going off the head of the dale.

"This was drawn in thirty-six," Hale said. "By whom?"

"A gentleman from Kendal way," Dent said. "He was here from the back end of April to about hay time — you'd call it seven weeks. He never walked the bound. It falls at Rogationtide, and he was at his desk that week."

"And after?"

"He went out. He never came back in." Dent looked at the fell and not at Hale. "My father was alive then. Nobody ever told the man why it wasn't confirmed. Nobody thought to."

Hale walked back to the inn with the plan under his arm and sat with his book by the candle, and went through it page by page, counting the names.

There were not many pages yet. Brock's hand was on every one of them but the first.
