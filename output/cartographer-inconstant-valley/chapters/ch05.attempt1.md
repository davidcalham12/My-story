# Chapter 5 — First Light on Raven Hause

The parlour fire at Nether Scarrow drew badly in a west wind, and there was a west wind. Brock had the jug and went round with it as though the room were his, which in the way that mattered it was. He filled Coulter's cup, and the carrier's, and Hale's, and came last to Nat Fowler, who had taken the settle end nearest the door and was drying his boots without appearing to.

"Martinmas will be on us before we've done with this," Brock said, pouring. "Your father's ground comes up then, doesn't it, Nat. The yearly." He said it the way a man says the weather is turning. "Hollins Hall likes a useful man. It always has."

Fowler said nothing. Hale, who had eleven pages of the day's work to reduce and a column that had refused him four times, heard the words go past him the way one hears a cart in the lane, and wrote *17 May, cont.* at the head of a clean leaf. Tenancies were nothing to him. He had never in his life owned a rood of anything.

He worked until the fire was ash. The sight from Sile Head was unfinished — a ray to the church tower struck and entered, and the second ray, the one that would close the triangle on the oak, still wanting. To break down the instrument now was to lose the whole setting and to begin again tomorrow with a new levelling, a new orientation, a new set of errors to argue with. He had lost four days to arguing with errors.

Nobody in the dale would have left an instrument out. He had been told so, pleasantly, by three separate people who had not quite said why. It was custom, and custom was not law, and law was what he was paid for. He climbed back up in the last of it with a blanket rolled under his arm, checked the plumb and the levels, laid a stone at each foot of the tripod, and sat down beside it in his coat.

The cloud came onto the fell about eleven. He did not sleep so much as hold still while the hours went over him. His boots filled. There was a moment somewhere in the small hours when the wet had gone through to his shirt and he thought quite clearly: *this is what the note at Midsummer costs, and it is not too much.* Then a long grey while of nothing, and then the shape of the theodolite standing above him against a sky that had begun to be a sky.

A light came up the fell, swinging, and went out.

"You'll have had a poor night of it," said Brock, out of the dark, from perhaps twenty yards. He had the lantern in his left hand with the flame just killed and Hale's field book under his right arm, flat against his coat to keep the wet off it. "I thought you'd want this. A man wakes up on a hill without his book and he's lost half of what he saw."

Hale got up. His knees were not his own. "You're early."

"I'm always early. It's the only economy I have." Brock was already looking east, to the hause, where the fell shoulder had begun to separate itself from the cloud. "It'll be on Raven Hause in a quarter of an hour. There's no sense standing in the dark when it's coming."

They walked across. Hale remembered that walk afterwards in every particular: the bracken soaking him to the thigh, the peat sucking at his heel on the second crossing of the runnel, the cold gone so far into his hands that he had to work them under his arms as he went, and Brock talking the whole way in that even, warm, procedural voice — the state of the Ingleford road, a dispute over a hog, what the Commission would want and in what order it would want it. He remembered coming up to the station under the boundary oak with the light standing on the fell, actually on it, the grass going from grey to a wet yellow-green around their boots and the oak putting a shadow west of itself for the first time that day. He remembered Fowler there with the flag already planted, and the Reverend Amos Dent a little apart with his hat in his hand and his coat buttoned to the chin, and thinking, with a distant professional satisfaction, that Brock had contrived to have two signing hands on a fellside at four in the morning, and that this was what the man was for.

He remembered the oak, and the tower away down the dale catching it, and the angle between them, which was an angle he could have taken half-asleep, and which was true; those two had closed on one another in every survey ever made.

He remembered the cold.

He did not remember the pen. But there was no moment at which he looked for it, so there was nothing, then, to miss.

By the middle of the morning he was back on Sile Head with the cloud lifting off it, and the theodolite stood where he had left it, every foot on its stone, the plumb bob hanging quiet. It was pointing at the church tower.

He stopped six feet short of it and did what he would have done anywhere on earth: he walked a circle round the tripod at two paces, and then at one, reading the ground. Cropped grass, wet, laid over from the night's weather, and laid all one way — the way the wind had gone. No heel. No toe. No print of a hand or a knee. Nothing dragged and nothing pressed. He knelt and put his face close to the near foot and the stone under it had not shifted in its bed; the moss round the stone was unbroken.

He looked along the telescope. The cross hairs sat on the tower, near enough, off perhaps by a hair to the north.

He wrote, standing, *Instrument found undisturbed in position, telescope bearing on Nether Scarrow tower. No marks.* Then he broke it down and carried it off the hill, and did not think about it, because there was nothing in his training that told him what to think.

It was evening, at the parlour table, going back through the book to reduce the week, that he came to a Day 9 entry he did not know he had.

It was in his own hand. Not an imitation of his hand — his hand, tired, the *R* of Raven with the long tail it got when he was cold, the figures cramped the way they always went when he wrote standing. An angle taken at the Raven Hause station, oak to tower, closed and entered. Below it, in the plain words he used for such things, the head of the parish carried to Raven Hause.

Beneath that, Nat Fowler's mark, witnessed and named. Beneath that, in a small upright clerical hand, *Amos Dent, Incumbent.*

Nowhere on the leaf was Silas Brock's name. Hale looked for it twice. Its absence troubled him and he could not have said, if asked, on what grounds.

He took up the pen to strike the entry through, and stopped with the nib an inch off the page, because a line drawn through a signed entry was a correction, and a correction was a new entry in every respect, and a new entry required two witnesses who had stood at that station for the taking of the correction itself, and the men who had signed the original counted for nothing toward it even if they were the same two men.

He put the pen down. Then he drew a loose sheet towards him and wrote the rule out in full, clause by clause, to see whether it would look different in his own writing.

It did not. It meant that he could not lawfully touch his own book until two people willing to sign stood with him under the oak.
