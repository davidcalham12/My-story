# Chapter 7 — Durations and Dead Men

The perambulation book had lain at the bottom of his own box for eleven days, under the spare arrows and a coil of whipcord, and on Sunday morning Hale took it out and set it on the parlour table at the inn and opened it because there was nothing else left to open.

It was not a survey. He had half expected the vicar's neat Durham hand to have smuggled some figures in, a chain here, a furlong there, and there were none. There were no figures at all. The bound of the parish of Nether Scarrow was recorded in how long it took to walk and in the names of men who had died.

*From the ford stone, the time of a short psalm to the thorn where Jonas Hird stood.* *From Hird's thorn, by the gate, the time of the Creed said through, to the sheepfold that was Wilfrid Tatham's.* *Above Tatham's fold, going wide of the three intakes, the time it takes to say the Lord's Prayer twice, to the great oak.*

Going wide of the three intakes. He turned back. 1789, another hand, browner ink: *out beyond the three intakes above the beck, as always*. 1751: *without the intakes*. 1703, the earliest, the letters cramped and the spelling loose, and there it was again — the walk went out round the ground above the beck and came back to the oak, and had done so in every hand for a hundred and forty years, and not one of those hands had written a single number to say how far out.

Hale sat with his thumb on the page for a long while. The Commission would not have called it evidence. The Commission would have called it folklore, and apportioned by estimate, and been wrong by three intakes. But it was signed, every walk of it, by the incumbent of the day, and the rule stood plain in the printed instructions in his own bag: no field outside the walked bound could be numbered, and the walked bound was what the incumbent signed.

The dale had kept the only record that could not be argued with, and had kept it in a form no surveyor would stoop to read.

He closed the book and went to find Fowler.

They went up together after the morning service, not speaking, the bracken wet through to his knees inside a hundred yards. The boundary oak at Raven Hause stood where it had always stood, and looked, as it always did, like a thing that had been put there rather than grown.

"Friday," Hale said. "First light. Tell me what you saw."

"Mr Brock came up," Fowler said. He was looking at the oak.

"Before Mr Brock came up. Was I awake?"

"You were on your feet, sir."

"That is not what I asked." Hale set the theodolite case down in the heather. "Did I speak to you? Did I give you an instruction — any instruction — before that entry was made?"

Fowler considered the oak. "You had your book," he said.

"I had my book." Hale heard his own voice go flat and did not trouble to mend it. "Nat. I am not asking you for a distance and I am not asking you to name a direction. I am asking you what you put your mark to and why you put it there."

The wind came over the hause and turned the bracken silver and turned it back.

"I put my mark to what was set in front of me," Fowler said. "The angle on the oak was true." He did not shift his eyes. "And Mr Brock had spoken of Martinmas the night before."

Hale waited for more, and there was no more, because there was no more. Fowler did not add that he was sorry. He did not say that his father was sixty-one and held his ground by the year and would not get another year anywhere in the hundred. He did not ask to be understood. He had given three facts, in order, and every one of them was true, and together they were worse than a confession would have been, because a confession could have been disputed.

"Thank you," Hale said.

"That's what's in your hand, sir," Fowler said. "I'll not say it for you."

Hale set up over the oak because there was nothing else to try and because he had woken with the thought and been unable to put it down. He levelled the plates, and swung, and brought the cross onto the church tower at Nether Scarrow, four miles down the dale and grey in the morning, and read, and wrote. He swung to the ford stone at the mouth of the Sile and read, and wrote. He closed the round back onto the tower.

The three angles closed on themselves to the second.

He read them again. They closed. He struck the instrument, reset it, and did the whole round a third time, and it closed, and he stood in the wet with his hand on the limb and something in his chest going over like a cart on a bank.

Eleven days. Eleven days of columns that would not sum twice alike, of the same wall measuring four different lengths between breakfast and dinner, of his certified chain telling him lies in good faith. And here were three things — a tower, a stone in a ford, an oak — that agreed with themselves and with each other and, he understood with absolute clarity, would agree for any man who ever came here with a glass, and had agreed for the four honest fools whose four honest plans disagreed about everything else. They had each measured a different dale. Their dales had answered them, and gone quiet, and told the next man something else. His own lines had stopped repeating the hour he rode out over Sile Ford and came back a stranger to his own arithmetic. That was all it was. He said it to himself once, flatly, and let it go.

And the instrument on Sile Head, turned in the dark to face the tower with no mark in the grass at its feet — he had taken that for a warning, and lain awake calling it one. It was not a warning. He had left it with a sight unfinished and it had settled, the way water settles into the beck, onto the one direction that was not in dispute. He understood it now as an instruction: look there. It was easier to think the thing had been telling him where to look all week than to think there was no reason in it at all, and he took the easier thought, knowing what he was doing, and stood there holding it while the wind came over.

Relief went through him, and directly behind the relief, cold as the ground, came the other thing: the dale had been answering him helpfully the whole time.

"I thought I should find you up," Brock said.

He came the last of the slope with his coat over his arm and his colour high, entirely pleasant, and out of his satchel he drew a roll of parchment and opened it on the flat stone by the oak with two pebbles to hold the corners. A fair copy. Engrossed — properly engrossed, an office hand, the columns ruled, the upper pastures set down as waste of the manor in a clerk's beautiful letters.

"It wants nothing but your seal," Brock said. "It would save you the Rogation walk, which is four hours of wet, and it would save you the delay, which I think you can afford less than I can. Your note falls at Midsummer, Hale. I do not say that to press you. I say it because it is true and we are both men of business."

Hale looked at the parchment for a while.

"No," he said.

Brock waited, warm, patient, a half-step ahead as always, for the reason.

Hale gave him none. He knelt and began to pack the theodolite into its case, and after a moment Brock rolled the copy up, carefully, so as not to crack the ink, and said something civil about the weather turning, and went down.

The light was going off Raven Hause when he reached the church, which meant it was going off everywhere at once, and Dent was in the porch with the key in his hand.

"Tomorrow," Hale said, "I will take a new entry at the station at the oak. Not a correction endorsed on the old one — a fresh taking, at the station, and two witnesses who have stood there for the taking of it, signing before I leave the ground. The men who signed on Friday count for nothing toward it. I want that understood by whoever comes."

Dent turned the key over in his fingers.

"I'll sign a bound I have walked," he said. "And I'll sign it once."

"Once is all I want."

The vicar locked the door and tried it, as he did every night, and looked up past the tower to where the fell had gone the colour of ash.

"We start," he said, "when the light is on the fell. Not before."
