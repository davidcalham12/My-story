# Chapter 6 — What Is Written Can Be Taken

She saw him from the barn door when he was still only a dark stroke on the track, and she knew him by the way he carried the book: under the arm, level, the way a man carries something he will not let get wet.

Hannah Rigg went in and set the kettle on the crane and swung it over. The fire had been banked since morning and wanted rousing, so she roused it. Her younger boy was at the trough and she told him to stop where he was and not to come in, and he did not ask why, because he had learned that much.

By the time the surveyor's boots came into the yard the water was singing. That was the whole of the courtesy, and it cost nothing, and she had been doing it to men like him since she was nineteen.

"Mrs Rigg."

"Mr Hale. Get that coat off before it gets into you."

He got it off. He was wet to the shoulders and had the look men got up here in their second week, when the cloud stopped being weather and started being a room they lived in. He sat where she pointed him, which was the settle by the fire and not the chair at the table, and he put the field book on his knee and not on her cloth.

She poured. He watched her pour, and she let him.

"I'm obliged to you," he said. "I'll not keep you above a quarter of an hour. I want the extent of your ground."

"There's a scone there, and butter."

"Thank you." He took one. He was hungry and trying not to be seen to be. "The extent of your ground, Mrs Rigg."

"My lads mow it in three days," she said, "if the weather holds and if the little one doesn't take a scythe to his own leg again."

He set the scone down. "That is a rate of work. It is not an extent."

"It's what I've got."

"Then put it another way for me. A man walking the top wall of it — how long is he at it?"

"He's at it from the milking to the milking, if he stops to look, and they always stop to look." She sat down across from him with her own cup and did not drink from it. "My husband used to do it before his breakfast. He was a quick walker and a poor eater."

Hale wrote nothing. He had a way of not writing that was as deliberate as writing. "I cannot enter three days' mowing in a field book."

"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose you can."

In her head it was seven acres and a rood in the Hollins intake, and eleven and a half in the Lang Riggs, and the little one under the scar was four and some perches she had never bothered to settle because her father had never bothered. She knew it the way she knew the stairs in the dark. It sat in her like a stone in a shoe and it did not come up her throat, and it never had, and she had long ago stopped noticing that it never had.

"There's a thing I'd tell you," she said instead, "while you've the tea in your hands."

"Say it."

"My eldest was up Thursday night into Friday with a ewe that was going to die and didn't. He was in and out of the low fold till the light came." She turned her cup a quarter round on the table. "He saw Mr Brock pass. Then he saw him pass again. Before the light, both times, and going by he had a lantern lit, which was fair enough, for it was black as a shut byre. Coming back it had greyed off enough that a body could see his own feet, and the lantern was still burning. That's what stuck in the lad. A careful man like that, burning oil he'd no use for."

Hale had gone very still, in the way of a man who has found something and does not yet know which pocket to put it in. "Before first light."

"Before it."

"Your son is certain of that."

"He's certain the ewe didn't die. The rest he's certain of the same road."

"Did he speak to him?"

"He did not. You don't, at that hour." She got up and lifted the kettle off. "I'm not telling you what it means, Mr Hale. I don't know what it means. I'm telling you the lad saw it and it's stuck in him, and I'd not have it stick in him for nothing."

He looked at the fire for a while. He did not write that down either, and she thought better of him for it. Then he said, "Thank you," and nothing more, and she was glad, because she had no more of it to give and did not want to be asked for what she hadn't.

"Come out," she said. "The rain's given over."

They went out through the top gate and along the wall with the beck talking away under them, and the cloud lifting off the shoulders the way it does at the end of a wet day, grudging, showing the ground in pieces. She took him to the first gate and stopped.

"That one's the Hollins intake. My grandfather's father took it in. That one beyond, with the lambing wall, is the Lang Riggs. And yon under the scar is the Little Waste, which is a joke, and the joke's on me now."

"They are good grass," Hale said.

"They're the only good grass I've got. Everything else is ground that grows stone."

"They're on no plan I have," he said. "Estate, enclosure, turnpike, county. Not one of the four."

"No," said Hannah Rigg. "They wouldn't be."

She put her hand on the gate and looked at the Lang Riggs, where the wall ran up and the light came along it, and she wanted to say to him: *stand there and I'll tell you it to the yard.* The wanting arrived whole and clear and went nowhere, the way water goes nowhere in a stopped spout. She went round it.

"Now hear what I'm asking you to hear," she said. "If the head of the parish is put at the oak, and the tops are entered waste of the manor — that's the words, waste of the manor, they'll be Mr Brock's words — then these three are not in the parish's fields at all. They're rough grazing, and the rough grazing is Hollins Hall's. My hay. My lads' three days. At Lady Day I'm a trespasser on it and they can have me off, and they'll do it kindly, with a letter, and they'll be sorry."

Hale said nothing for a moment. Then: "Then they must be numbered."

"Numbered."

"There is no other remedy. The Commission confirms nothing that lies outside the bound as walked at Rogationtide and signed by the incumbent. A field outside that walk cannot be numbered at all — not by me, not by anyone after me. Inside it, I can number them. But understand what that is, Mrs Rigg." He turned to face her. "Numbered is tithed. It is a figure in a book at Somerset House, and a figure on a plan with a seal on it, and it is there after you and after your sons and after their sons. It does not come off again. Your people have kept this dale out of that for a hundred years, and I am telling you plainly that I would be the man who puts you in it."

She thought of Amos Dent then, and the chest, and the book in it, and how easily a woman might ask. And she thought what asking costs, and who ends up holding the other end of it, and she set the thought down where she had set it down before.

"Number them," she said.

He looked at her. "You are sure."

"I'm forty-six and I've buried a husband and I can count," said Hannah Rigg. "Number them."

She left him at the gate and went back down for the kettle, because it would want filling again before the lads came in. At the door she turned. The three of them lay above the beck in the last of the light, green as anything in England, and there was nothing written on them anywhere in England at all.
