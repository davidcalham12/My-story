# Chapter 4 — The Width of a Field

The mare took the ford at a walk, picking her way over the stones with the water breaking white at her fetlocks, and Hale did not look down at the ford stone as he passed it. He had taken it against the church tower eleven times in five days, one fixed thing sighted to another, and it had closed the same every time. It was the one thing in Scarrowdale he no longer had to think about.

On the far bank he turned in the saddle.

The dale lay behind him in the afternoon light, flat and ordinary. That was the whole of it. From inside, Scarrowdale was a thing of shoulders and hollows, of ground that rose when you expected it to fall, of walls that ran away from a man at angles he had to take twice; from here it was a green wedge between two grey fells with a beck down the middle and a church tower at the foot of it, and it looked no stranger than Wyresdale or Roeburndale or any of the twenty valleys he had chained for the turnpike trust. Cloud sat on the fell shoulders as it always sat. Smoke stood up from Nether Scarrow in three thin columns. He could have covered the whole parish with his thumb.

He thought: it is a small place and I have made too much of it. Then he turned the mare's head for Ingleford and rode on, and did not think of it again until Wednesday.

At Ingleford the standard lay in the market hall, a brass-ended bar bedded in stone that the borough surveyor kept under a hasp, and the borough surveyor was a fat courteous man named Hetherington who had been comparing chains for nineteen years and said so twice. They laid Hale's Gunter's out along the flags. Hetherington went down it link by link with his thumb on the brass, telling the hundred off under his breath, and tried the tally-marks at ten and twenty and fifty, and stretched it, and slackened it, and stretched it again.

"True," he said. "True to the link. You've a good chain, Mr Hale, and it's been kept dry."

"I'd like that written."

"You'd like it written." Hetherington seemed pleased rather than put out, and went to the desk, and wrote it out in a clerk's round hand — *compared this sixteenth day of May 1843 against the borough standard and found true* — and signed it and let the ink dry and did not blot it. Hale folded it once and put it in his breast pocket, against the interim return he had already posted, and felt the small square weight of it there for the rest of the day. A man who can prove his chain is a man who cannot be told his chain is wrong. He slept at the Gate, ate a mutton chop, and thought about the low pasture line with a satisfaction he would have been embarrassed to have named aloud.

He came back over Sile Ford on the Wednesday at three, with the light still high.

Fowler was at the lane corner with Coulter and the arrows, because Fowler was always where he had been told to be. Hale said they would run the low pasture again — the same line, the lane corner to the elbow of the beck — and that he wanted it before the light went. Fowler nodded and took the leading end.

The chain went out. Coulter held; Fowler, leading, set the arrow in the ground at his chain-length and went on, and Coulter drew it as he came up to it; the bracken hissed on the links. Hale walked the line at Coulter's shoulder as he had walked it three times before, and knew the ground now, the tussock at the fourth chain and the soft place where the run-off crossed. At the elbow Fowler stopped and stood, and drew the last arrows out of the ground on his way back, and came and set them in Hale's palm without a word, and Hale counted them in his own hand and read the tally at the chain and took the links himself off the brass.

Eleven chains, seventy-two links.

He stood with the arrows in his hand and the sun on the back of his neck and said, quite evenly, "Again."

They ran it again. Ten chains, ninety-six links.

"Again."

Eleven chains, forty links.

The light was going off Raven Hause when they ran it the fourth time, the shadow coming down the fell face like water finding a level, and Hale made them go fast and did not let Coulter stop to shake the kinks out. Twelve chains, eleven links.

He sat down at the station on the flat stone by the lane corner and opened the field book on his knee.

*TEN CHAINS, FIFTY-THREE LINKS*, in his own iron-gall, Friday's entry, signed at the station by Brock and by Coulter, and under it Monday's two repetitions in his own hand, identical to the link, and the margin.

He did not enter the afternoon's four. He was checking, not recording; he knew the difference, and there was no one at that station to witness a thing taken there but Coulter, and a man does not enter four contradictions into a book he must seal. He set them down on the back of the Ingleford certificate instead, where they had no standing and could do no harm, and added them.

Eleven seventy-two. Ten ninety-six. Eleven forty. Twelve eleven. He added them in the order taken and got forty-six chains, nineteen links, and divided and got a mean of eleven chains, fifty-five links, which was a number belonging to nothing on the ground. He added them backwards to check the carrying and made it forty-six chains, nineteen links again, which proved only that he could add. He grouped them in pairs and the pairs did not agree with each other. He struck out the longest and the shortest and the two that were left were eleven seventy-two and eleven forty and there was no honest way to call those one line. Whatever he did to the column, it would not sum twice alike into anything a man could put on a plan, because four figures that differ are four lines, and he had chained one field.

The chain was true. He had it in writing in his pocket, in a clerk's round hand, from a fat man in Ingleford who had done it for nineteen years.

Fowler coiled the chain. He did it the way he always did it, laying the links over his palm in figures of eight and thumbing them flat, and when it was coiled he set it down by Hale's boot and set the ten arrows beside it in a bundle with the rings together, tidily, within reach of Hale's hand. He had been on the leading end for every run of the afternoon and for the Friday line before it. He looked once at the book open on Hale's knee, and once at Hale, and there was nothing in his face that could be called surprise.

Then he said good evening, sir, and went down the lane to Nether Scarrow, and did not say one word about the figures.

By candlelight in his room at the inn at Nether Scarrow, Hale turned back to Monday's page and read his own margin note through twice. *The dale's reputation for crooked measure is slander, and every man who came before me was careless.* His hand, his ink, at the station, with the light good.

Nothing in his book might be struck through. A correction was a new entry in every respect, and wanted two witnesses who had stood for the taking of the correction itself, and he had no correction to offer them.

He left it standing and went to bed.
