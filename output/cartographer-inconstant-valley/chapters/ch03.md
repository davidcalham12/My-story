# Chapter 3 — Identical to the Link

Sunday came up dry, which in that country was an event, and the cloud that had sat on the fell shoulders since Thursday lifted off them like a lid taken from a pot. Hale walked down to Nether Scarrow church with his boots still stiff from the beck and sat at the back, where a surveyor belongs, and where the whole congregation could be counted without turning his head. Forty-one souls. He counted them twice and got forty-one both times, and thought nothing of it at the time except that it was pleasant to be somewhere the numbers behaved.

Amos Dent preached on the Rogation. It was the obvious sermon with the week ahead of them, and he gave it plainly, without oratory, standing square and speaking as though to men he would see later in the day, which he would.

"We go out after the second lesson," he said, "and we are back before the bread has gone cold. There's the length of it. We stop where Josiah Thwaite stopped, and we say the Creed there as he said it, and we go on. Then there's a stretch that takes about as long again, and at the end of that stretch is the place my father put his hand on when I was a lad of nine, and I have put my hand on it every year since, and I shall put my hand on it a week tomorrow. We are asking a blessing on the grass. The grass knows where it is. It's us that has to be reminded."

Hale, who had been trained to hear a description, sat forward.

There was not a direction in it. Not a north, not a south, not a right hand or a left; no up the dale and no down it. There was not a distance in it either — no rood, no chain, no furlong, not so much as a good step. The whole bound of a parish had been laid out from the pulpit in the time it took a clock to move a quarter, and it had been laid out entirely in how long a thing took and who had last done it. Hale caught himself waiting for the number the way one waits for the second boot to drop, and it never dropped, and nobody else in the church appeared to notice its absence. A boy of about eleven in the third pew was nodding along at the right places. He knew where the place my father put his hand on was. Every one of the forty-one knew.

It was, Hale decided, a charming and entirely intelligible country manner, and he was glad of it. A parish that carried its bound in its head like a catechism was a parish that had not let its bound wander. He would put the numbers to it himself and the two accounts would agree, and then they could have the Creed and he could have his plan.

Brock was waiting at the lychgate with two shaved larch poles across his shoulder and a lantern he would not need, having come down at first light on the chance.

"I'd not ask a man to labour on a Sunday," Hale said.

"A pole's not labour, Mr. Hale, it's a mark." Brock shifted the larch and smiled. "You'll want a second hand on the entry, and I'm stood here already. The lad's up at the Hall with the flag."

They set the first pole in the peat under the boundary oak at Raven Hause, guyed with three cords and packed with stone, and the second on Sile Head, where the wind had scoured the ground down to grit and the setting took an hour. The air was so clear that Hale could see the whitewash on a byre a full two miles off. He took the instrument to the station he had chosen on the rough ground between, levelled it, and read the angle between the two poles on both verniers, then reversed the telescope and read it again, and the four readings lay within nine seconds of one another, which on that ground with that wind was better than he had any right to. He wrote it in iron-gall ink at the station with the book on his knee. Brock signed under it. Coulter, the Ingleford lad, climbed up from the flag with his cap off and signed under Brock, and Hale blotted the page and only then stood, and only then took the theodolite off its legs and cased it. The poles he left standing. Poles were marks, as Brock said, and marks were meant to stand.

Coming down he was given tea at three farms in turn, at three kitchen tables scrubbed pale, and at the last of them, the high one under the Hause where a widow kept the best grass he had seen in the dale, a scone still too hot to hold. He asked, at each, how far it was to the next house along, out of nothing but the habit of the trade. At the first they told him it was the pipe of a pipe. At the second, that Robert Hird used to do it before his tea went cold and none of them had ever managed it since. At the third the widow considered him for a moment and said her elder boy did it in the time it took her to skim the milk, and would he take another.

He went back to his lodging warm and pleased and did not think about it again.

On the Monday he took the chain out alone. It was slow work single-handed — set the arrow, walk back, sight to the plumb-line, drag the chain forward, and do it again eleven times — and it took him from eight until past two to run the low pasture line he had chained on the Friday with Fowler leading. He knew it was no legal measurement. There were no witnesses at the station, and a figure without two hands to it was a figure for his own satisfaction and nothing else. He wanted his own satisfaction very badly.

Ten chains, fifty-three links.

He stood in the wet grass and looked at the arrows in his fist and felt something come loose in his chest. Then he coiled up and walked it a second time, from the far end back, to take out any bias of his own pacing.

Ten chains, fifty-three links. Identical to the link.

Fowler came down off the intake at five and took the chain from him without being asked and began coiling it in the long practised loops. Hale opened the field book on the wall top and wrote in the margin, in ink, in his own hand:

*Re-measured this day by myself alone, twice, and agreeing exactly. The 1836 plan is 47 links in error over this line. The dale's reputation for crooked measure is slander. Every man who came before me was careless, and I am not.*

He blotted it. Fowler stood with the coiled chain over his forearm and watched him do it, and looked at the page a moment longer than he needed to, and said nothing at all, and went home.

The evening stayed dry. Hale sat up an hour with the book and carried Friday's line and Sunday's angle into the column, and the column summed, and summed again the same when he ran it upward to check. He put out the candle and slept the whole night through without waking once.
