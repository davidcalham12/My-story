# Chapter 1 — The Ford Stone

The cart came down out of the cloud at the pace of a walking horse, and Edmund Hale saw the dale open beneath him the way a ledger opens: narrow, ruled, and full of entries he had not yet read.

At the mouth of it the beck ran shallow over gravel, and in the gravel stood a stone. It was perhaps four feet out of the water, grey, its top worn to a saddle by a century of cartwheels finding it and shying off. The carter stopped without being asked.

"Sile Ford," he said, which was a name and not a distance, and Hale noted that he said it without complaint.

Hale got down into the water. It came over his boots and he did not mind. He had the chain off the cart before the carter had finished settling the horse, and he walked the first length himself, the brass handles cold, the arrows in his left hand, counting under his breath because there was no one here to count for him. From the stone to the bend of the lane above the ford: two chains, sixty-eight links. He wrote it on his cuff and then, disliking himself for it, copied it nowhere until he had a table.

The satchel held four plans and he spread them on the tailboard with a stone on each corner. The estate plan, drawn for Hollins Hall in a fine tilted hand. The enclosure plan of 1836, which had never been confirmed and did not say why. The turnpike survey, which cared only about the road and had measured everything near it twice. The county plan, which cared about nothing and had measured accordingly.

The ford stone stood on all four. From the stone to the church tower at Nether Scarrow, the estate plan gave a distance; so did the enclosure; so did the turnpike; the county map, being coarser, gave it scaled. Hale laid his dividers across each in turn, and each in turn gave him the same figure within the thickness of the line.

He did that three times. Then he sat on the tailboard with his boots dripping and looked at the four plans, because from the ford stone onward they disagreed about everything else. The Sile did not run the same course on any two of them. A wall that the estate plan ran east of a barn ran west of it on the turnpike. Field corners wandered. Only the stone stayed where it had been put.

"A good mark," he said aloud, and was pleased, because a survey wants a datum and here was one that four men had already agreed upon without meaning to.

---

The lane up was steep and streaming, bracken wet to the knee, cloud sitting on both shoulders of the fell like something laid there. Half a mile up — he thought half a mile; he would chain it — he overtook a woman with a yoke and two milk cans, walking without apparent effort.

"Good day. How far is it to Nether Scarrow?"

"You'll be there before the milk's warm," she said pleasantly.

"I meant in miles."

"It's the same walk my mother did with these cans, and she wasn't quick."

He tried it a third way. "Would you call it more than a mile, or less?"

"I'd call it a walk," she said. "Tom Braithwaite walked it last that wasn't from here, and he wasn't put out by it."

"Is the church straight on, or does the lane turn?"

"The lane goes where it's always gone." She shifted the yoke to her other shoulder and smiled at him with real warmth, as though he had been kind to her. "You'll see the tower when you're meant to."

He stopped asking. It was not rudeness; that was the thing he turned over for the next quarter hour. She had answered every time. She had simply never once given him a number or a bearing, and she had done it the way a person breathes.

---

Nether Scarrow was eleven houses, an inn, and a church whose tower was older than anything else in sight of it. Amos Dent met him in the porch with his coat already on, as if he had been expecting somebody for some days and had not known which day.

The chest under the north window took two keys and a shoulder. Dent lifted out a book bound in calf gone the colour of wet slate, and put it into Hale's hands with both of his, the way a man passes something heavy rather than something precious.

"The perambulation book," he said. "It's yours to keep while you're here. I'd rather it were in the parish than in the Hall."

Hale opened it. The hand was various and old. He read: *thence by the Creed's length to the thorn where Robert Hebblethwaite stood* — and then a gap, and another hand, and *from the thorn to the water as long as a man says the Lord's Prayer twice, John Rigg last walking it*.

"There are no distances in it."

"There's the walk in it," Dent said.

"Will you read me the head of the parish out of it? I'd have it in your voice for the book."

Dent looked at the page for a while. "It's the length of the Creed at a walk, up from the thorn," he said, "and my father walked it last."

"That isn't a direction, Mr. Dent."

"No," the vicar agreed, without any embarrassment at all, and locked the empty chest.

---

In the inn parlour that evening Hale laid the four plans side by side on the long table and set a candle at each end, and looked at four different rivers.

The Sile on the estate plan came down the eastern side of the flat ground below the Hause. On the enclosure plan it came down the western. The turnpike had it crossing itself, which it could not do. The county plan had it as a blue thought.

He took out the field book and mixed nothing, the iron-gall being already made up, and wrote in a hand he was proud of:

*11 May 1843. Sile Ford. Ford stone to bend of lane, 2 ch. 68 lks., taken by me. Ford stone agrees with all four plans in relation to the church tower. Nothing else does.*

Then, because a man should know his own position as well as his parish's, he reckoned on the endpaper. Midsummer. The note on the theodolite and the chain fell due at Midsummer, and it was the eleventh of May, and that gave him forty-four days to have a plan sealed, or he would be a bankrupt owning a very good instrument.

The lantern came down the dale at nine, swinging, and Silas Brock came in on the cold with it.

"Mr. Hale. Brock, for Hollins Hall." He had the landlord's attention before he had finished his own name, and ordered supper for two. "You'll have found nobody to witness you yet."

"I've been here five hours."

"That's about how long it takes." Brock sat, warm and entirely easy. "I'm not dale-born, sir. Lancaster, same as yourself, they tell me. Which means I can sign what I've stood over, and I'll stand over anything you like at any hour you care to name. Dawn suits me. So does dark."

"I'll want two hands on every entry."

"You'll want a second, and I'm stood here already." Brock cut his meat. "The estate has always understood the head of the parish to be at Raven Hause. I only say it so you know where we think it is. You'll find it where you find it."

He went at eleven. Hale watched the lantern climb until it was a low star going up the fell, then turned back the field book, and wrote in the margin, small:

*check ford stone again on leaving.*
