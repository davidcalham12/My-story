# Chapter 8 — The Walked Bound

They came out of the church porch at eight with the cloud sitting low on the fell shoulders, and Amos Dent went first with the perambulation book open across his forearms like a man carrying water.

He read as he walked. Hale had expected mumbling and got none: Dent's voice went out over the wet bracken clear enough to reach the back of the line, where the Ingleford lad Coulter carried the arrows in a bundle because nobody would let him count them aloud. *From the ford stone, the time it takes to say the Creed twice, to the thorn that Simeon Rigg's father grubbed out and his son set again. From the thorn, the time it takes a woman to fill a kist with peat, to the intake wall that Joseph Dent kept.* Names of dead men, and how long the walking took, and not once in two hours a distance.

Hale walked at Dent's shoulder with his field book buttoned under his coat and understood at last, with a kind of vertigo, that there was nothing in the book for Dent to break himself on. A hundred and forty years of hands had kept it so. They had written the bound in durations and in the dead precisely so that it could be said out loud by people who could not say a distance, and Dent reading it to the whole parish was not an exception to the dale's silence. It was the silence working, in public, on its one appointed morning.

Nat Fowler walked in the body of the line with his hat off. Nobody spoke to him and nobody kept away from him. His father's tenancy came up at Martinmas whatever happened on this hill.

At the top of the dale, where the track bent in under the scree towards Raven Hause, Hannah Rigg went straight on.

She did not announce it. She simply held her line where the path turned, out along the lip above the beck, round the Hollins intake and the Lang Riggs and the Little Waste, three green shelves of hay in a country of rush and stone. Dent read on without a check. Behind him the line bent, sixty souls of Scarrowdale turning out wide after her through wet grass to the knee, and not one voice said that this was not the way. Brock walked it with them, at the back, hands behind him, watching the ground.

It took forty minutes longer than the old way. Hale counted nothing and said nothing, and let them walk.

At the boundary oak they stopped. It stood as it had always stood, and it had measured the same for every surveyor who had ever put a glass on it, and it would measure the same for the next.

Rigg came up to the trunk and laid her right hand flat against the bark, fingers spread, and held it there.

She did not look at him. She did not say a word. Hale understood what he was being given and went back twenty yards to the instrument without thanking her for it, because thanking her would have made it a favour, and set the theodolite, and took the angle off the back of her hand where it lay on the oak. Her arm did not tremble. It was raining lightly. He read the vernier, and read it again, and wrote the figure standing there, and she took her hand down and shook the wet off it and went to stand with her sons.

Then the station at Raven Hause, with the light still on the fell.

He was careful out loud, for the benefit of anybody who might one day read the sheet. This was a fresh taking, here, now: he said so. He shot the round again from this station — tower, ford stone, oak — with Rigg standing at his left elbow and Dent at his right, and both of them stayed through the whole of it, the setting, the sighting, the booking. The Friday entry was lawful and would not be scratched out. He wrote a new one under it in iron-gall, that the head of the parish stood at the boundary oak by the bound as walked at Rogationtide this day, and that the three intakes above the beck lay within it, and he did not lift his hand until it was finished.

Rigg signed. Dent signed. They signed as witnesses who had stood at that station for the taking of this entry and nothing else; Dent's Friday signature counted for nothing toward it and Fowler's mark counted for nothing toward it, and Hale did not ask Fowler to come near the book. He watched them both put pen down before he touched the tripod. The light was still up on the fell above them, greying, but up.

He carried the instrument off Raven Hause in the dusk with his hands shaking, and the shaking was not fear.

Tuesday he began again from nothing. Church tower, ford stone, boundary oak: the three that hold, angles taken directly among them and no other line trusted to carry weight. Everything else in Scarrowdale he hung off that triangle like washing off a rope. He worked from six until four in the vestry with the plan weighted down by candlesticks, numbering every field that lay inside the bound as the parish had walked it — and the Hollins intake, the Lang Riggs and the Little Waste were inside it, and so they could be numbered, and being numbered they would be tithed, and being tithed they were Hannah Rigg's beyond any agent's argument forever.

He added the columns at half past four. He added them again at five. The two totals were the same.

He sat with that a while. It was the first honest sum he had made since he rode out over Sile Ford, and what it bought was the dale on paper at last, every green shelf of it, numbered in a book at Somerset House that would outlive her and outlive her sons.

Dent signed the bound he had walked. Once.

Brock was the first man to his hand. He came across the churchyard with his hat already off, said it was a good plan and honestly got, shook once, and settled the survey's bills at the inn to the penny before Hale's horse was brought round — chainmen, candles, the lad's boots, everything. He did not complain. He did not look up the dale.

Hale rode out at seven with the fee assured and the note answerable at Midsummer.

At Sile Ford he stopped and did the only thing left to him that meant anything, and measured the stone. Four feet two inches and a half above the water: as near as his first guess had put it on the day he rode in, and exactly what it would give anybody who ever asked it. The three constants did not care who held the chain.

Everything else he had drawn was true, and sealed, and would stand — and would never measure so again for him, because he had gone out once already and come back, and the dale did not offer that twice. If he ever rode in over this ford, not one line of it would repeat.

He turned the horse up the Ingleford road and did not look back.
