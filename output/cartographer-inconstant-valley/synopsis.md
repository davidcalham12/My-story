In 1843, Edmund Hale rides into Scarrowdale to do arithmetic: number every field, seal the plan, collect the fee already owed against his instruments. Four existing surveys of the dale agree on nothing except three fixed points, and no one born here will give him a distance or a bearing — only how long the walk takes, and who last walked it. His chainman hands him the count arrow by arrow rather than speak it aloud. Hale takes the silence for stubbornness, measures the ford stone against the church tower, and rides out satisfied that the dale's reputation is slander. He is wrong before he reaches Ingleford. He returns to find a line he chained twice himself now giving him a different figure on every run, a field corner missing that no one recalls ever being there, and, worst of all, an entry in his own field book, properly witnessed, carrying the parish boundary somewhere he does not remember standing to put it. Silas Brock, the estate's agent, is endlessly obliging about witnessing whatever Hale writes. Hannah Rigg, holding the last good grass under Raven Hause, is the first to tell him plainly what his signature is worth to her. Nothing in Scarrowdale threatens him. Tea is poured. Doors open. The horror is in the columns, and in what a courteous valley can make a careful man believe he wrote himself. A novella about surveys, silence, and the ground a name can take.

**For readers of** *The Loney* by Andrew Michael Hurley, *Piranesi* by Susanna Clarke and Sarah Perry's *The Essex Serpent*.
