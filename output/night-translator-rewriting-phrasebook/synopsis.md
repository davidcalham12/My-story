Eleven years, Irena Vasko has read the frontier from the same chained book: left column the state's tongue, right column the traveller's, nothing said that is not printed and numbered, every deviation entered in ink as an unlisted utterance. It is a post that runs on the promise that the phrasebook cannot change — only misread — and Vasko has built her whole discipline, and her silence, on that promise holding. It costs her the ordinary reflex of speaking a sentence that isn't hers to read; she keeps a private tally of everything the book will not let her say. Then, on a night no different from eleven years of them, a traveller named Ana Petrik crosses outbound at 01:40, and at page seventy-four Vasko finds a sixth phrase where five have always stood — printed in the traveller's column, addressed, unmistakably, to the person behind the counter. No rule has been broken. No one has written in the book. Duty officer Tomas Brandt logs the moment exactly as procedure demands and asks nothing further; that is the kindness he believes in. Vasko says the words as printed. Petrik answers as though she had been waiting for them. What follows asks only what any night shift can survive: who is changing a book no one at the post may touch, and why the changes are learning to speak about her. For readers of *Convenience Store Woman* and *The Employees*, who want their uncanny delivered in triplicate and countersigned.
