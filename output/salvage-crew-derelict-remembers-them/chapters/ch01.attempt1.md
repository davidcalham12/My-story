# Chapter 1 — Right Berths

The hull came up out of the drift without a beacon, without a running light, without anything at all, and Priya Sen read the figures off her slate while Nell Varga held the Grebe steady on the hook.

"Drift rate's point-oh-four. No spin worth naming. Skin temperature says it's been cold — properly cold, not a season. Decades." She scrolled. "Longer than any of us have been alive, this thing's been out here."

Nobody answered that. The hook took with a sound she felt in her back teeth.

Inside, the derelict had the same bones as the Grebe: the same narrow throat of a corridor, the same lip you learned to step over, the same four berths off the same square of deck. Priya's lamp swung across it and she thought, without wanting to, *somebody built these to a pattern*, and that was fine, that was ordinary, ships came in patterns.

The bunk tags were card, slotted into brass frames beside each berth.

VARGA. Top left, where Nell slept.

WHITLOCK. Below it.

SEN. Opposite, third along, hers.

Nobody had said a word aboard the wreck. Not one. Priya was certain of it, the way you are certain of a thing you will go over later and still be certain of.

The fourth frame was in the corner, in the place where the fourth frame was on their ship.

None of them went near it. Priya's lamp found it anyway, because a lamp goes where you look, and the four letters sat there in the cold being perfectly legible.

"Corey Dunn," Abe Whitlock said.

He said it flat, the way he said *spool line* or *feed pressure*, and then he let it sit, and the silence afterward had a shape to it. Priya looked at the bulkhead. So did Nell. The bulkhead was grey and had a weld seam running up it and was a very good thing to look at.

Abe was already unshipping the torch. He'd started before the tags were finished being read.

"Take the corridor plating first," he said, in his own time. "Frames after. Then the berth deck skin, then whatever's left of the hold. That's the order. Don't want the deck coming out from under the frames." Hose, striker, regulator. "Same as always."

Priya brought the tag gun up and put number one into the bulkhead plate beside the seam, a hard punch and a numbered disc biting steel, because nothing was salvage until it was tagged and she needed to hear something happen.

Over the corner berth, where no one was standing, the cabin light came up. Warm, low, steady. A light for reading by.

"Eight hours forty on this shift," Nell said. "Keep cutting."
