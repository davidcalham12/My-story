# Chapter 1 — The Question

Leo woke on his tenth birthday with Captain Crunch still in his hand.

He had fallen asleep holding the fossil the night before—a small, spiral shell pressed into stone, the color of butter. He had found it at Cadaques one summer ago among the rocks, and named it right then, staring at the shape of it, the way the spiral turned inward like a secret. Captain Crunch was proof that things under the sea had been real, and then dead, and then turned to stone, and now were in his hand. If that was possible, Leo thought, then anything was possible.

He lay in bed for a moment, letting the morning light come warm through his window. Ten. He was ten today. He could feel it already—something small and important, like a stone in his pocket that weighed more than it should.

From downstairs, he heard Alice moving in the kitchen. Pans. Water running. Something that smelled like cinnamon.

Bruno pushed open the bedroom door with his nose. The terrier was small and the color of rust, and he had developed, over the course of his life as Leo's dog, an absolutely certain opinion about when breakfast was supposed to happen. He trotted directly to the bed, climbed onto Leo's chest with his front paws, and fixed Leo with the expression of someone who had waited long enough and was prepared to be patient for exactly one more minute.

"All right," Leo said. "I'm getting up."

The cardboard observatory still stood in the garden where Leo and Samuel had built it two summers ago. That was what Leo thought about as he came downstairs, Bruno at his heels: his grandfather in the garden, measuring corners, explaining why a roof needed four walls underneath it or the wind would have opinions about where it went.

Alice was at the kitchen table with her hands around a cup of tea. Samuel was there too, in his loose sweater with the pocket that never failed to hold something useful—string, or a pencil, or a nut he had found and thought interesting.

"Happy birthday," Alice said, and she and Samuel both stood up.

"Happy birthday," Samuel said, and they sang it, badly and with the same voices they always used, which made Leo want to laugh and cringe at the same time. Bruno barked once in the middle, as though he thought they needed conducting.

There was bread and jam, and a small cake that Alice had made, chocolate with her thumbprint still visible on one side where she had rested her hand while she was frosting it. Leo could see the whorl of her fingerprint, and it made the cake better somehow, to know exactly where her hand had been.

As they were clearing the plates, Leo asked the question.

"What is on the other side of the hill?"

Alice and Samuel exchanged a look that Leo had seen before. It was not unkind. It was only as though they were checking something with each other, some small agreement.

"More countryside," Alice said.

"More of the same," Samuel added. "Fields. Another village a few miles on. The road that goes to it."

"Has anyone been there?" Leo asked.

"People must have," Alice said. She was standing at the sink, running water. "The road goes somewhere."

"But has anyone we know been there?"

Alice paused. Water ran over her hands. When she turned back to Leo, he saw it clearly: the moment when she realized she could not answer the question, and the moment after, when she had already stopped trying to.

"No one has bothered," she said finally. "It doesn't really matter what's there, Leo. It's just more of the same as here. There's no reason to go looking."

Samuel had his hands in his pockets. His eyebrows were doing something complicated.

"When you're older," he said, "you'll understand that the best things are usually close to home."

Leo didn't ask again. He took his plate to the sink and let it go.

But he had seen it in their faces: the not-bothering. They had looked at the hill once, a long time ago, and decided it was too much trouble. And then they had decided it was too much trouble again, and again, until they had stopped looking at all. It was the same way Alice had stopped rearranging the bookshelf, or Samuel had stopped building impossible things in the garden. Leo could see it happening while he watched them. They were still themselves, but a part of them had decided to stay still.

That afternoon, Leo climbed into the cardboard observatory.

The walls had begun to soften at the corners where the rain came in each autumn, and the roof's seam had a gentle sag to it, but it was still a proper structure. Samuel had been right about the four walls. Leo had to crouch to get through the door, and once he was inside, the light came greenish through the observation slots they had cut and the holes in the paper walls that had simply worn through.

Bruno trotted in behind him, circled twice, and then folded himself onto the floor beside Leo's feet. The terrier had a habit of doing this—appearing to sleep in Leo's presence while somehow remaining aware of everything Leo did. It was a gift he had. Now he sighed, a small, decisive sigh, as though the afternoon had been long and Leo's sitting down had finally put things right.

Leo arranged his stones on the shelf they had built. He had found them over the course of the past summer, collected them because of the weight of them in his hand, or because one side was smooth enough to be almost slippery. One was the color of the sea, even though it had come from the road. Another was so heavy it pulled his pocket down when he carried it. The rest were arranged by color, by the size of them, by the path he had walked when he found them. His mind was very quiet when he was arranging them. The world made sense in the weight of stones.

He lifted his head and looked out through the observation slot toward the hill.

It rose beyond the fence at the garden's edge, a long sloping thing covered in grass and smaller stones of its own, rising up into the sun. Beyond the top of it, where the grass gave way to sky, was what he wanted to know about. Not magic. Nothing that didn't make sense. Just what was there. What he hadn't seen yet.

The afternoon was warm. Bees were moving in the flowers near the fence. Everything smelled like summer—like grass that had been in the sun, like dust in the air, like something green growing.

And then, with the sun still high and warm on the cardboard walls, and Bruno snoring quietly beside him, Leo admitted something to himself that he hadn't quite said before: he was afraid of the dark that fell between here and there. The dark at the foot of the hill, and the dark that would come when evening arrived, and the dark that might come if he ever got close enough to see what was beyond.

He could picture it exactly. The moment when the light left his eyes and he couldn't see his own feet. The moment when the world became only sound, and uncertainty, and the fast beating of his heart.

He did not tell Alice. He did not tell Samuel. He did not even tell Bruno, though Bruno already seemed to know most things.

Bruno shifted in his sleep and pressed harder against Leo's leg, as though he was dreaming of something that required being held down. The dog's paw twitched. He might have been chasing something, or running toward it, or running away.

Leo put his hand on the dog's warm side and looked out at the hill a little longer, until the sun moved far enough that the light changed on the observatory walls, and the shadows of the fence posts grew longer on the grass, and the afternoon began its slow turn toward evening.
