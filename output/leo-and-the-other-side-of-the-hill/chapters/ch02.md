# Chapter 2 — The Torch

The kitchen drawer stuck when Leo pulled it. It always did, catching halfway out and requiring him to jiggle it sideways. Inside were forks and spoons and the spatulas Alice used for cooking, and underneath them, pushed to the very back where nothing good ever lived, were the matches.

At least, that was where Leo expected to find them. Instead, his fingers closed on something else—something hard and cylindrical and covered in dust. He pulled it out.

It was a torch. An old one, the kind with a heavy metal casing and a red button on the handle. Leo had not seen it before.

"Alice?" he called.

"In the garden," her voice came back, faint through the open window.

Leo turned the torch over in his hands. The casing was dented. The lens was scratched. But when he pressed the button, the bulb inside flickered once, twice, and then held steady with a thin, yellowish glow.

The battery was dying. That much was obvious. The light was hardly brighter than a candle, and not as warm. But it worked.

Leo found himself smiling. He pressed the button again. Off. On. The beam appeared and disappeared on the kitchen table. It made a small circle on the wood, pale but real.

He set the torch down carefully and returned to the drawer. He found the matches too, tucked behind a rubber band and some old batteries that had once belonged to something else. He carried both to the observatory after breakfast, though he only meant to light the oil lamp.

The torch he kept.

Three days later, Leo switched off the lights in the kitchen.

He did not turn them off on purpose at first. He had been at the sink, washing the breakfast things, thinking about nothing much, when Bruno—his brown terrier with the crooked ear—decided that the hem of Leo's shorts needed immediate investigation. Bruno nosed at it, then bit it, then dragged backward as though it were a rope he meant to rescue from the sea.

"Bruno, stop," Leo said, but he was laughing.

Bruno yanked again, and Leo's knee bumped the light switch.

Everything went dark.

For a moment, Leo stood still. His eyes had been on Bruno, on the dishwater, on ordinary morning things. Now there was nothing to see. The kitchen was a place he had eaten a thousand breakfasts in, and now it might have been anywhere. His fingers tightened on the sponge.

His heart was louder than it should have been.

He could not see his own feet. That was the thing that made it real somehow—not the darkness itself, but the fact that part of his own body had vanished into it.

His hand was shaking. Not a little. Properly shaking.

Bruno whined and pressed against his leg. Bruno did not like the dark either.

Leo's other hand moved without him deciding it should. He found the torch on the counter where he had left it that morning. His fingers found the button. He pressed it.

The beam came on—thin, pale, a ghost of the bright thing a torch was supposed to be. But it found his feet. There they were. Real. Attached to him. The light caught the tiles beneath them and the edge of the sink and Bruno's nose, turned up toward the beam as though it were something to smell.

Leo's shaking hand stopped.

Not right away. Not like magic. But it stopped. He could feel it stopping, the panic letting go like it had been holding his bones in a fist and had finally decided it was bored.

"Captain Crunch," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the dark and the small light.

Bruno looked up at him, confused.

"That's what I found," Leo said, quieter now. "At the beach. That fossil. I found that myself."

He switched the light back on—the normal light, the electric light that banished all the dark into the corners. Bruno shook himself as though he had just come out of the rain.

Every afternoon in the first week of July, Leo took the torch to the hill.

He did not climb it. Not yet. He walked to the edge of the village where the neat gardens stopped and the longer grass started, to the place where the path became rougher and the shadows between the wheat-stalks grew longer as the sun slid westward.

The torch stayed in his pocket.

During the day, in the bright afternoons with the sky empty and blue, he did not need it. The sun was everywhere, pressing down warm on his head and his shoulders, lighting everything twice over with the light itself and the light that bounced off the grass. But he carried it anyway, and each afternoon he took it out and tested it, pointing the beam at the long shadows that stretched down the slope.

The light did not reach very far. In the bright sun, he could barely see where the beam ended. But it was there.

"It works," he said to Bruno on the first afternoon.

Bruno was not impressed. Bruno was more interested in a beetle that was crossing the path with a lot of important purpose and very little speed.

On the second afternoon, Leo climbed thirty steps up the slope and turned the beam on against the shadows. The beam looked stronger when he was higher up. The village below him shrank. The hill curved away in both directions, and for a moment Leo could pretend he was looking at a country he had never seen before instead of the very edge of the one he lived in.

On the third afternoon, Alice came looking for him.

"Leo!" she called from the village side. "Tea!"

He switched off the torch and turned around. The sun was still high, at least an hour from leaving the sky. No danger of breaking the rule about dusk. Still, he did not tell Alice about the torch. He put it in his pocket and went home.

On the fourth afternoon, Bruno refused to come with him.

Leo whistled at the gate, the way he always did. Bruno looked up from the garden, where he had dug a hole for absolutely no reason Leo could determine, and did not move. His ears were up. He was interested in the hole, not in the hill.

"Come on, Bruno," Leo called.

Bruno lay down in the hole.

"Fine," Leo said. He could not leave Bruno alone—Bruno might follow and get into trouble—so he sat at the garden's edge with his arm across the terrier's back, and waited while Bruno napped in his inexplicable hole. The sun pressed down. A butterfly landed on the fence post. Leo watched it more intently than he had watched anything in weeks.

When Bruno woke, he woke suddenly, stood up, shook, and trotted toward the hill with such determination that Leo had to hurry to keep up. Some days it took two of you to figure out who was supposed to be leading.

By the end of the week, he had climbed to the place where the slope grew steeper and the grass grew wild, all yellow-green and bent from the wind. He had tested the torch beam against the shadows there seven times. Seven times it had come on. Seven times it had thrown its small circle of light onto the grass and the rocks and the steep place that fell away beyond.

Seven times it had not let him down.

That was when Leo began, for the first time since his birthday, to think that maybe there was something on the other side worth finding out about. And maybe—just maybe—he was the person who was supposed to find it out.

He held the torch in his hand and looked at the beam in the afternoon sun, barely visible, and thought: it will have to be enough.
