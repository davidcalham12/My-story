# Chapter 3 — The Challenge

Iris caught him coming out of the village shop. He was holding a bottle of lemonade that was already getting warm. His thinking face was on—the one where his eyes looked at the hill even though the rest of him was looking at the pavement. Iris knew that face.

"You're going to try it," she said.

"Try what?" Leo said, but he knew.

"The hill. You've finally decided you're going."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Your face decided," Iris said. She was smaller than Leo by half a head, which made the pointing worse. "You stare at it every day. You ask people what's there. And you won't go yourself because you're scared."

Leo's whole face went hot. "I'm not scared of a hill."

"Then go now," Iris said. "Go right now. Go today at two o'clock and prove it."

Leo didn't answer. Bruno had walked over and fallen asleep on his foot.

"You won't," Iris said. "That's the thing. You're so scared you won't even try."

"I will," Leo said. The words came out before he could catch them. "This afternoon. Two o'clock. I'm going."

"I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Yes," Iris said. "I want to see which one it is—you turning back, or you being brave. I want to see."

They cycled out at exactly two o'clock. The old bicycle Alice had lent him was fast, and Leo liked the way it felt beneath him—solid, ready to go. Iris had her own bicycle. They rode side by side at first, then the path grew too narrow for that, and Leo pulled ahead without meaning to. Bruno trotted alongside them, tongue out, happy about it all. The afternoon was warm. The path was smooth and worn from other people's feet, and Leo could see the marks of their shoes in the dust. It went past the edge of the village and kept going, and with every turn it seemed to carry him further from the things he knew.

"How much further?" Iris called.

They had been cycling for three minutes.

"Almost there," Leo said, which was not true, but it made him feel braver.

The path got narrower. Leo had to concentrate on staying straight. The grass on both sides grew taller and wilder—tall enough that he couldn't see over it to the village anymore. Tall enough that if he fell, it would swallow him. His stomach felt very strange, the way it felt right before he was sick, except he wasn't sick, he was just scared. His legs were pumping the pedals but they didn't feel like his legs. They felt like something else was making them go. He hadn't brought the torch. He was going to climb a hill in daylight and he was afraid of the dark and he hadn't brought the torch. The thought made him pedal faster.

Then the path stopped.

There was a gate.

The gate was green and real and it went all the way across. The fence ran on in both directions until it disappeared into trees. On the far side of the gate, the path continued. The hill was there. Leo could see the slope of it, grass that looked even taller and wilder than the grass here. He could see where he wanted to go. It was right there.

A man stood beside the gate, and he was watching them.

He was the kind of tall that filled up the whole space where the path should go. His shirt was brown and dirty. His arms were long. His face was the colour of old brick, and it didn't move when he saw them. He didn't wave. He didn't say hello. He just watched. He was exactly the kind of adult who made children small. And he was standing between Leo and the hill like he had been waiting there all afternoon for someone exactly like Leo to ride up on a borrowed bicycle with his friend and a dog, and now his moment had come.

"Off," he said.

Just that one word.

"We were just—" Leo tried.

"Off my land," the man said. His voice was flat and certain. "You. The boy. The dog. The bicycles. Off. Now."

Leo understood that this was Mr. Pike the way you understand things in dreams. Just knowing. The farmer. The one who owned.

Bruno had stopped at the fence. He didn't bark or push against it. He just stopped. He could smell what a fence meant. Bruno's ears went flat. He put his feet down carefully, the way he did when the storm was coming, and he looked at Leo instead of at the gate, waiting to know what Leo was going to do.

Leo turned the bicycle around.

"Go on," Mr. Pike said.

They cycled back the way they had come. Leo's hands were shaking on the handlebars. He couldn't seem to make them stop. Bruno trotted alongside them without any argument, the way he did when the world was still good, when things made sense. His tongue was out. He looked happy. He looked like none of that had happened at all.

Iris rode beside him. She didn't speak. She didn't laugh at him. She didn't tell him he'd been right about the scared part, which was somehow worse than if she had. She just rode, and Leo could see her face in the corner of his eye—very still, very serious, and he couldn't tell if she was thinking that he was a coward or if she was thinking something else entirely. He wanted to say something. His mouth opened twice. Nothing came out. What could he say? That he was going to try again? He wasn't. That the farmer looked mean? He did, but saying so wouldn't change it. That he didn't understand why he was so frightened of a man who just wanted him off his land? He didn't understand it either. So he said nothing, and neither did Iris, and they cycled all the way back through the village in silence.

When they got to where the roads split, where Iris's house lay one direction and Leo's lay the other, Iris got off her bicycle. She stood with one foot on the ground, her hand on the handlebar. She looked at Leo for a moment—a real look, not the corner-of-the-eye look from the ride. Then she turned and walked her bicycle home without saying goodbye.

Leo put the bicycle away in the shed. He walked to the cardboard observatory in the garden. He sat down inside it with Bruno. He didn't tell anyone what had happened. He didn't make a plan to come back. He didn't say anything at all.

Bruno fell asleep against his leg. Leo sat in the dimness under the cardboard walls and waited for supper time.
