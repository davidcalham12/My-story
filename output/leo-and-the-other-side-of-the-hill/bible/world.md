# The World: Leo and the Other Side of the Hill

Leo's village sits in a small fold of countryside, close enough to the coast that sometimes the wind carries salt and the smell of seaweed up from Cadaques. The summers are long and warm, and the hill that rises at the village's edge has been there long enough that the grown-ups have stopped wondering what lies beyond it. But Leo has not. Every afternoon in June and July, when the grown-ups are occupied with their own errands and the light holds steady, Leo looks at that hill from the garden where the cardboard observatory still stands beside his grandfather's shed, and he asks: What is on the other side?

## Factions

- **Leo** — wants to climb to the hilltop and discover what the other side looks like; driven by the same curiosity that made him name his fossil Captain Crunch and collect smooth stones by their shape and weight.

- **The grown-ups in Leo's household and the village** — want to keep Leo close, safe, home before dusk, occupied with small tasks and the proper routes of the village. They do not climb the hill because it is not worth the trouble; they assume Leo will come to the same conclusion.

- **The other children** — want to be included, or to prove Leo wrong, or to claim the discovery for themselves. Their wants conflict with each other and with Leo's in small ways: some will follow, some will tell, some will try to turn back.

- **The farmer who owns the land on the hill's far side** — wants his property kept clear of trespassers and his gates respected. He will not be bribed or reasoned with; he will be faced.

## Means

- **The cardboard observatory** — built with his grandfather, still standing in the garden. It will not leave the garden, but it is the place where Leo's mind goes to imagine what he cannot yet see.

- **Leo's stone collection and how he sorts it** — by smoothness, by colour, by the paths they came from. His method of understanding the world through close attention to small things.

- **Captain Crunch, the fossil Leo found at Cadaques** — proof that wonder exists, that a beach holds questions worth asking. The reason Leo knows that something worthwhile waits to be discovered.

- **Bruno, Leo's terrier** — Leo's companion, will go anywhere Leo goes. His nose works better than Leo's; his courage is steadier. He will flinch at gates and boundaries, but only because he knows what they mean.

- **A bicycle** — fast enough to matter, small enough to hide. The difference between an afternoon walk and an expedition that reaches the hilltop before dusk.

- **The torch with a fading battery** — Leo's tool against the dark he fears. It works, but dimly, and he does not know how much longer it will shine.

## Rules

- **R1.** Dusk is the moment when the light leaves the sky entirely; Leo is expected to be home by then. If Leo comes home after dusk, Alice will ground him for the next day.

- **R2.** Mr. Pike guards the gate at the boundary of his farm and will not allow anyone to cross there. The fence surrounding his land has gaps to the north and south where it has fallen; crossing through a gap is trespassing, and Leo knows this when he does it.

- **R3.** Every time Leo enters a space dark enough that he cannot see his own feet—whether a barn, a shed, or the open land after dusk—he must light the torch and name aloud one thing he has found or noticed himself before going in. If he cannot bring himself to name something aloud, he cannot go forward with the torch lit.

- **R4.** Bruno will follow Leo anywhere, but he will not cross a fence or gate without Leo's hand on his collar and clear permission.

- **R5.** The village children know the near side of the hill; none of them have been to the other side, and none can tell him what he will find.

- **R6.** The torch grows dimmer with each minute it is lit; it never brightens again and will never be replaced. Nobody in this book can measure how much light it has remaining.

## Texture

The world is the size of a summer afternoon, all warm grass and long shadows and the small sounds of things moving in the hedgerows. It is safe enough that a ten-year-old is allowed to wander with his dog, but not so safe that he can pretend he's not afraid of the dark—or that he doesn't know the difference between the village he knows and the hill he does not. Everything is real: the bicycle is a real bicycle, the stone is a real stone, the farmer's anger at trespassing will be real. The wonders are real too, but small—a lizard on a rock, a path that goes where he did not expect, the way his grandfather's voice sounds when he explains something. It is the kind of summer where a child learns that the world is bigger than his village and more full of things worth noticing than anyone had told him.
