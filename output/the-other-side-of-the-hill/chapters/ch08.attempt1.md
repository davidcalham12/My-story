# Chapter 8 — Marcos Knew

Marcos walked into the kitchen as [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] was halfway through describing the fossil—the pale spiral Clara had let him hold, how it turned in the light like a secret the riverbed had been keeping for a thousand years. His mother was pouring milk into the blue pitcher, the one with a chip on the rim and still the most beautiful thing on the shelf. His father sat at the head of the table with his chin in his hand, which is what he did when he was listening hard, when he wanted to hear every word and the spaces between them. Bruno lay under the table with his chin resting on [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s foot, one ear tipped toward that voice, because Bruno could hear the things that mattered in it, could tell when [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] was speaking from somewhere deep and true.

Marcos stopped in the doorway.

He stood there in his good coat, the walking stick in his right hand, the one with the brass knob that had belonged to his own father. He didn't move. He simply looked at [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO], and [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] looked back, and for a moment he forgot entirely what he was saying, forgot the words in his mouth like they had scattered across the floor.

"He spent the night on the other side of the hill," his father said.

It was the plainest thing his father could have said, and he said it plainly, because his father never wasted words. But Marcos—something crossed Marcos's face like a door opening to the light. Not surprise. Not shock. It was the look of a person who had been waiting for a knock and finally heard it, and had always known exactly who it would be.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] watched it happen. He watched his grandfather's grey eyes stay exactly the same colour and brightness, watched something behind them shift, settle, become still.

"There is a girl there," [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] said. His voice came out smaller than he meant it to. "And her grandmother. Elena—the girl—she's twelve. She gave me—" He stopped. "Marcos, did you know Elena was there?"

Bruno's tail thumped once against the floor, because he knew that tone, that pause, the way [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s fingers went to the pocket of his trousers where he'd kept the smooth stone Elena had given him.

Marcos tapped his walking stick twice against the floor.

The sound was very clear in the kitchen. Tap. Tap.

"I have known Elena's house was there since before you were born," Marcos said. His voice was not what it usually was. It was simple, the way his father's voice was simple. "I wanted you to find it yourself, and you did."

One sentence. No story. No walking back to tell it sideways.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] felt his chest go hot, then tight. He felt his fingers curl around the edge of the table. He'd been frightened on that hill. He'd been lost. He'd stood on it in the failing light, and he hadn't known if he could trust Bruno with the way down, and he had trusted Bruno anyway because there was nobody else to trust. And Marcos knew. Marcos had always known. He'd known when he gave [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] the notebook for questions. He'd known when he tapped his stick and told the story about the wall.

"You knew," he said. Not a question this time.

"Yes."

It was the plainness that made [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] angry—not the knowing itself, but the plainness that said he was old enough now for the truth without dressing, without stories. It was Marcos admitting, in that single sentence, that sometimes a ten-year-old needs a direct answer, that circling isn't always enough, that the people who love you sometimes have to let you be frightened if they're certain you'll come through it brave.

Bruno pressed his whole body against [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s leg under the table, warm and solid, and [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] pressed back. He reached into his pocket and turned one of the smooth stones with his thumb, because sometimes you needed something to hold while everything else was changing.

His mother reached across the table and moved the butter dish an inch. It was about as much as she had ever said about anything, which meant it was quite a lot.

"You might have told me," [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] said. The anger was still there, bright and small and honest.

Marcos didn't move. He didn't sit down. His walking stick stood on the floor between him and the table, and his hand rested quiet on the brass knob.

"If I had told you," he said, and [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could see it cost him something—could see it in the pause, in the way his grandfather's jaw tightened and then let go—"you would not have gone. And I think you needed to go. I think you needed to find your own reason to be brave, not because I told you to be, but because you had to be."

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] opened his mouth. He closed it. He thought about the moment on the hill, when he had stood in the last light and told Bruno aloud that he didn't know the way back. He thought about Elena, coming out into the yard in her apron. He thought about the fossil, and the table with a place laid for him, and falling asleep in a stone house on the other side of a hill that nobody talked about.

He thought about not going.

If Marcos had told him, he wouldn't have gone.

Bruno came out from under the table and walked three complete circles before lying down directly on [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s foot again. That was Bruno's way of saying that things were confusing and frightening, but that he was staying right there anyway, which is more than a lot of people could do. Bruno's quick sharp bark came next—not happy exactly, but not sad either. The bark that said: *We are here, aren't we? We made it.*

Marcos set his walking stick against the wall, the way he always did, and pulled out his chair.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] didn't look away from his grandfather. He met his eyes—the grey ones that had been waiting for this moment since before he was born—and found in them what had always been there: a man who believed in him so completely that he would let him be frightened to prove it.
