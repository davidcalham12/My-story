# Chapter 5 — The Climb

The sun was high and the world was blue. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] and Bruno set out after breakfast while the air was still cool and the whole day lay ahead of them like a promise. The stone path was clear beneath his feet, the leather of his shoes worn smooth by his own walking, and Bruno trotted beside him with his nose down, reading the morning in the dust.

They climbed steadily. The village fell away below them, small and ordinary, rooftops and gardens and the ribbon of the stream. The pine shadows grew longer as they rose, and the air changed—thinner, clearer, the kind of air that made him feel he could see everything. He could see the fountain where Rosa said goodbye. He could see the ovens where she was working instead of being here. He had promised to tell her everything.

Past the shepherd's markers, the path began to disappear.

The stones were still there, worn by feet and weather, but they came and went now, and between them was loose shale and low brush. He had to watch where he placed each step. Bruno was still beside him, but something in the dog's manner had changed. He stayed closer, his usual trotting urgency gone. When they paused in a stretch of deep shade beneath an overhang of rock, Bruno moved against [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s leg and whined—a thin, sharp sound that cut through the afternoon stillness.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] stopped. His breath was coming shorter than it should, his palms suddenly damp. The stones around him felt loud under his feet, as if they might give way. He turned smooth pebbles over in his hands, the way he did when thinking, and understood something true about himself:

It was not the dark he was afraid of.

"Bruno," he said aloud, and his voice sounded strange in the high air. "I'm frightened. Not of the dark. Of being lost here. Of not knowing the way back down and not being able to bring you home."

The dog leaned against his shin. His small body was warm. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] thought of his mother, of his father, of the table where they would all sit at supper—and of the empty chair that would be his, the plate kept warm. He thought of Bruno's leash, which the dog had fetched himself on Day 2, which meant Bruno knew they were going somewhere that mattered.

He went on.

The climb sharpened. The fear did not leave—it sat in his ribs like something that had made a nest there—but he climbed anyway. One foot, then the other. Bruno stayed pressed against him, his breath quick and anxious, but he did not stop. He did not refuse.

And then, without warning, the ground leveled.

They stepped out into light and space, and the fear fell away.

Before them lay a whole valley that did not exist from the village. There was a stream, silver in the afternoon sun, and fields that went down toward a stone house with a wall around it. The house was small and solid and real. In the yard, someone was standing with one hand over her eyes, looking up at the hill.

Looking at him.

She was a girl, perhaps twelve, with dark hair and a brown dress. She stood motionless, working something out, and then she called across the distance. Her voice reached him thinned and strange, as if traveling miles, though the valley was not wide—or was it? It sounded both nearer and farther than he could tell.

"Who are you?"

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] opened his mouth to answer. Behind him, the light had changed.

The sun was touching the horizon.

He felt the rule arrive—the one his father had given him—and he understood it differently than he had before. It did not say run. It said stop. It said that wherever you are when the sun touches the edge of the world, that is where you stay until daylight returns. And that staying is not a failure. That is obedience.

He had not broken his father's rule. He was keeping it now.

"We can't come down," the girl called, and her voice came both nearer and farther still. "It's not safe to come down now. You'll have to stay."

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] looked down at Bruno, who had stopped trembling. He looked across the valley at the girl in the yard, at the light spilling from a doorway behind her.

The girl reached down and took Bruno by the collar with one small hand, certain and gentle.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, both of you."

She led them toward the light.
