# Chapter 10 — The Other Side Now

That evening, after supper had cleared and the light was turning long and gold across the garden, Leo found his father in the kitchen, moving slowly the way people do in heat, and asked him the question he had been holding since breakfast.

"Can I climb the hill again? Soon? With the same rule—home before dark?"

His father looked at him for a long moment, and then smiled. "Yes," he said. "The same rule. And you'll follow it?"

"I will," Leo said, and he meant it the way he had on the day he first asked.

He was outside on the back path, heading toward the garden wall, when Rosa found him at the gate. She must have been waiting there, flour still in the creases of her sleeves, her hair coming loose from where she had pinned it that morning.

"There you are," she said, and her words tumbled out all at once. "I've been waiting since my mother let me go—I told her you'd be home by now, you had to be home by now, or she would have sent someone to look. What happened? What did you find? Did you see something? Is it as strange as everyone says, or is it just rocks and nothing? Tell me everything, you promised you would tell me everything."

She laughed at herself, embarrassed, and brushed flour from her sleeves without noticing she was doing it.

So he told her. About the path that got harder to see. About Bruno pressed against his leg, whining. About the thyme that rose in a smell when you trod on it, and the quick grey birds calling from places you couldn't locate. About how steep it was, steeper than it looked from below. About reaching the crest and seeing the valley spread out below like a secret the hill had been keeping. About Elena, who was twelve and had a house there with her grandmother, and a fossil of her own—pale and spiral-shaped—sitting on a shelf like Captain Crunch sat on his. About how the sun touched the horizon and he had to stop, so he stayed, and the dark was not the thing he had been afraid of after all. About waking early and climbing down in the grey light, Elena steady beside him and Bruno between them.

Rosa's eyes grew wider with each thing he said.

"You stayed there all night?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And you came back the next morning?"

"Yes."

"And Elena—is she kind?"

"Yes," he said. "She collects things the earth leaves behind. Like I do."

Rosa nodded slowly, turning this over in her mind the way she turned flour between her fingers. Then she said, "Next time you go, can I come?"

"Yes," he said, and knew his father would say yes too.

He found Marcos in the kitchen, moving slowly, the way the grandfather moved through almost everything. The old man looked at him without speaking for a long moment.

He could see it already—Elena standing in the doorway of the cardboard box, looking up at the painted stars. But it wasn't only his to show her. It was half Marcos's. He took a breath.

"Can I show Elena the observatory?" he asked aloud.

Marcos tapped his stick twice. And then he spoke plainly, in one sentence, the way people speak who have decided that a young person has earned the truth:

"Yes—she will want to see what you have made of the light."

Together, they walked out into the garden as the evening began to change color, Marcos moving slowly with his stick, and Leo keeping pace beside him. The cardboard observatory stood where it always stood, near the back wall, midnight blue and creaking faintly in the warm breeze. Up close, you could see all the places where the boxes had been taped together, where seams showed underneath the paint like old scars. The pencil marks where eight-year-old Leo had drawn the stars were still there—some in the right places, some not quite, some doubling back on themselves because he had not been sure.

Marcos opened the door, and they went inside.

It smelled like it always smelled: dust and pencil shavings and something like the memory of cardboard. The polished glass lens caught the last of the sun and threw it across the small interior, and the cardboard tube that had been meant for a telescope stood waiting, patient and crooked in a way that made it look kind.

Leo climbed onto the low shelf and Marcos handed him Captain Crunch—the fossil he had found two summers ago at Cadaques, shaped like a sleeping thing, smooth from the sea. The boy placed it on the highest shelf where the light would catch it, there where the best of the evening sun fell.

Then he climbed down and brought the jar of stones from where he kept it, and one by one he and Marcos arranged them on the shelf beside the fossil. The amber river stone glowed where it caught the light. The smooth river stones sorted themselves by color: white and rust and amber. The black stone from the beach caught the sun and held it like a secret.

Marcos stood quiet, looking at what they had made.

Outside the open door, Bruno had circled three times in the dust of the garden path and was now asleep, his chin resting on his paws, the warmth of the day still in his fur. The cicadas had begun their evening song. The sky above the garden was turning that particular blue that comes just before dusk, the blue that holds gold around its edges.

Leo stood beside his grandfather, and together they looked at the light on the fossil and the stones, the way it moved across the shell-shape of Captain Crunch, the way the amber stone held the warmth of the last sun. Somewhere, on the other side of the hill, Elena was probably looking at the same sky.

The stars were beginning to appear, faint and patient, waiting for the last of the light to leave.

Marcos did not speak. Neither did the boy. There was nothing that needed saying.

Outside, the evening deepened, and the garden held them both.
