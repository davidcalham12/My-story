# Chapter 7 — The Return

The kitchen door was open and both of them were standing in the yard.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could see them from half a field away. His mother had her arms crossed, which she did when she was waiting, and his father was still, the way he went when something had happened that nobody could fix yet. The morning sun was already high—too high. He had meant to be home when it rose.

He could not read their faces until he was closer. Fear was the first thing. Then something else underneath it that might have been anger, or might have been something harder to name. When he was near enough to see their eyes, he stopped walking.

"I kept the rule," he said.

His mother made a sound that was not quite a word. His father did not move.

"The sun touched the horizon and I was on the other side of the hill. I stopped right there and I waited for the morning, and I came home in daylight, like the rule says. I kept it."

His mother crossed the yard in three strides and gripped his arm. She did not say anything. His father stepped forward and put a hand on his shoulder, and [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could feel it shaking very slightly.

"Start from the beginning," his mother said into the top of his head.

So he did.

"The path is narrower than we thought. Me and Bruno. We went up after breakfast, when the sun was high and we had the whole day ahead of us, and the path kept going down and down, and then I couldn't hear the village sounds anymore, and I said to Bruno that I wasn't sure which way was back, and that was scary because he was trusting me, and I couldn't let him down, so we just—we just kept going forward because stopping didn't help. And then the path got better and wider and we could see there was a house down in the valley, a real stone house that nobody ever talks about, and there was a girl there, Elena, she's twelve, and she was in the yard, and the sun was right on the horizon and I just—I had to stop. Right there. And that's where I—"

"You slept there," his father said. It was not a question.

"Yes. Elena and her grandmother Clara. They had supper and I told them about our hill and Bruno pressed against me the whole time, frightened, and Elena has this fossil—a spiral, pale as a river stone, smooth like something the water had worked on—and she found it in the riverbed, and I had Captain Crunch so I set him next to the fossil and we looked at them together for a moment, and I left Elena one of my smooth stones—the striped one—to keep. And in the morning we walked back down and the sun was coming up and when we got to the foot of the hill Elena turned back and I came home."

"Your mother didn't sleep," his father said quietly.

"That's what the rule says. Stop when it touches the horizon. I stopped. I waited for the light to come back. I came home in the light."

He felt his father's hand leave his shoulder. He heard the scrape of the kitchen chair as his father pulled it back and sat down hard. His father put his hands flat on the table, and they were still now, and his father was looking at them the way he looked at things he was trying to work out in his head.

"Neither did I," his mother said. There was something in her voice that was not anger. It was worse than anger. It was the sound of someone who had been afraid for a very long time and was only just putting it down.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] smelled something then. He turned his head and saw it: his place at the table, set as it always was. There was a plate on top of it, and on top of that plate another plate, turned upside down to keep the warmth in. The cup beside it had gone cold.

He did not know how long it had been sitting there. He did not want to count.

"That was your supper," his mother said. "From last night. We kept it warm."

She said it the way she said things that were simple facts, but it was the most terrible fact he had heard. They had waited through the night. They had made supper at the hour they always did, and set his place as if he would be home, and kept the food warm all through the dark. And every hour the plate sat there, getting older. All through the hours when they could not sleep—when his mother stood at the window looking toward the hill and the invisible path beyond, and his father walked the floor—they said almost nothing to each other because there was nothing left to say. They had sat down to eat at the usual hour and left his plate there like a promise.

His father's hands were still flat on the table. His eyes were still on them. Then slowly, very slowly, he lifted his eyes to [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s face.

And nodded.

Just once. A small nod, held for a second, and then his father looked back at his hands.

Bruno pressed against [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s legs, the way he did when he needed to be close to somebody, needing the feeling of someone there beside him. His mother finally loosened her grip. Bruno trotted straight to his food bowl and began to eat so quickly his whole back end waggled with the effort, and then he came back and barked once, sharp and happy, because he had a story to tell too and it came out all at once in the language of dogs—the whole hill and the scary path and Elena's yard and a house he had never smelled before and the strange kitchen corner where he circled and finally lay down and walking back in the sunrise—all of it at ankle height, told in quick sharp barks and the way he pressed close and circled three times before lying down on [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s feet.

"Tell us about Elena," she said. "Tell us about the house. Tell us everything, and take your time."

His mother's hand was back in his hair, not moving, just there.
