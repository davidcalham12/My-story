# Chapter 4 — The Lower Path

The afternoon was already warm and the light falling sideways through the olive trees when Leo slipped out of the house with Bruno at his heels. The canvas bag was slung across his shoulder—his six stones, Captain Crunch wrapped in the handkerchief, and the leather notebook Marcos had given him, waiting to be filled with answers. Or questions. He was still not quite sure which one he was supposed to be collecting.

He had made it as far as the edge of the village plaza when Rosa stepped out from behind the baker's awning, her apron still white with flour, and caught his elbow.

"You're going to the hill," she said. Not a question.

"Reconnaissance," he said, which sounded better than "wandering around and hoping to get scared."

"Well, I can't come tomorrow." The words came out so fast he almost missed them. She brushed flour from her sleeve without looking down, a habit so automatic her hand did it before her brain caught up. "Mother's got the ovens going tomorrow for the feast and someone has to mind the counter and the market rush, and I'm someone, apparently, which is extremely unfair because you know I could climb the hill just as well as you, probably better, actually, I've been thinking about it all night—"

"Rosa."

"—and I can't because of bread." She laughed, but it was the laugh that meant she was disappointed. "Bread is ruining my life. So you have to promise. Absolutely promise. You have to tell me everything you see. Everything. The color of the stones, what the path looks like, if it's steep, if there are birds, if anything is weird or strange or—" She brushed her sleeve again, harder, and a little cloud of flour rose into the light. "Does it smell like anything?"

"I haven't been there yet."

"Well, when you do. You have to tell me absolutely everything." She was looking at him seriously now, underneath the tumble of words. "Promise."

He promised.

"Because I can't ask my father anymore," she said, quieter. "He just keeps saying it's less interesting than I think, and I don't believe him, and he knows I don't believe him, so now we just don't talk about it." She shook her head. "I'll have flour in my hair by tonight and my feet will hurt. But you—you get to actually go."

He wanted to say something to make it better, but Rosa was already brushing past him toward the bakery, her apron leaving a little ghost of flour in the air. Bruno whined and pressed against his leg.

"I know," Leo said. "It's not fair."

The path to the foot of the hill was one he knew well—past the last houses, past the stone wall where the village ended, across a stretch of scrubland where the earth was pale and hard and wanted nothing from anybody. The sun was already beginning its slow tilt toward the west, still high enough that the sky was still blue, but the heat was different now, less fierce, gentler. The light was shifting. He could feel it happening, the world changing from the bright hammer of midday into something more golden, more slanting. Bruno walked close, his ears up, sniffing at the stones and the dry grass as if each one was a message left for his nose alone.

When they reached the base of the hill proper, where the slope began to rise, the ground changed under their feet. The path here was not one that anyone had made on purpose—it was older than that, worn into the hillside by years of feet and hooves and weather, generations of creatures climbing and climbing and leaving their marks in the earth. It was marked with stones, small ones placed as if someone was saying: this way, follow this. Leo could see it clearly, the route stitched up the slope in pale markers against the darker earth, deliberate as a thread sewn by patient hands.

He started to climb.

The first stretch was easy enough. The stones were solid under his boots, and the slope was steep but not terrible. Bruno climbed easily at his side, his paws sure on the path, his tongue lolling slightly with the pleasure of work. The air changed as they went higher—cooler, touched with the smell of wild thyme that grew between the stones, bruised green and sharp and bright. It smelled like the hill wanted you to know it was alive, wanted you to know it was there. His legs felt strong, and his breathing was steady, and Bruno's ears stayed up and happy, twitching at every sound.

They passed the first stands of pine trees, where the needles had made the earth soft and dark and rich. The shade was welcome after the exposed slope. He could see the path continuing up and up, marked by those patient stones, and his heart was beating faster—not from fear, he told himself, but from the climb, from the work of it. Longer shadows stretching across the stones like dark fingers.

Higher still, the path began to turn—up and around the curve of the hill toward whatever lay beyond. That was when the ground changed again. The loose stones became more common here, shale and flint that wanted to slide under his boots with every step. The path ahead of him narrowed, and shadow pooled across it in thick places. The pine trees grew thicker, their needles blocking out the sky, and the light took on that peculiar quality of places where the sun couldn't reach directly anymore—dimmer, cooler, uncertain.

He stopped.

The path ahead looked both tempting and wrong—tempting because it continued, clearly marked with stones, inviting him on, and wrong because the afternoon was already turning and he was only supposed to have so much daylight. He was doing the arithmetic in his head, feeling the numbers in his chest: sun touching the horizon around here meant he needed to be back at the house, home, safe, and if he kept going now he might not be. The numbers didn't work. The loose shale underfoot was treacherous, and the pine needles had made this stretch too dim to trust. And anyway, he thought, there was always tomorrow.

Bruno stopped when Leo stopped, ears still up, alert, not worried. The dog didn't want to turn back from anything—his ears had stayed happy all the way up, happy still. He simply waited for Leo to decide.

"Come on," he said to Bruno, and they turned around.

The dog followed without hesitation, without any reluctance, as though they had been planning this return all along. His ears stayed up all the way down, swiveling at the sounds of insects and birds. When they reached the foot of the hill again, the light had begun in earnest to change. The sun was still above the horizon, still visible between the trees and buildings of the village, but it was clearly heading toward the edge of the world. The shadows had grown long and purposeful, pointing like fingers toward home. Toward supper. Toward his mother asking him where he had been.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow I do this alone.
