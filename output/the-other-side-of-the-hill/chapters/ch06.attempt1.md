# Chapter 6 — On the Other Side

The kitchen was smaller than [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s own kitchen at home, and warmer. There was a wooden table that looked as if it had been serving supper in the same room for fifty years, and Elena's grandmother moved around it without fuss, setting down bread and cheese and a pot of soup that steamed as if it had been waiting for someone all along. The stone walls were the same grey as the walls at home, rough and honest, and in the window above the sink someone had left a smooth pebble on the sill—just one, turning slightly in the last of the daylight.

"Sit," Elena's grandmother said, and it was not a question. Her name was Clara, and she had the same quiet voice as her granddaughter, but older, with the kind of patience that comes from a life lived slowly. "The boy will be hungry."

He was. Bruno circled the kitchen floor three times, each circle smaller than the last, before collapsing with a sigh into the corner nearest the table. When Clara set a bowl of broth in front of him, he ate without speaking, and the bread was dark and still warm from the oven. There were no questions asked about where he had come from or why he had arrived at sunset with a scruffy dog and a nervous voice.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] kept thinking about his own table, three valleys away. His plate was laid. His place was waiting. Rosa would be clearing the other dishes now, and his father would be reading in the window, and Marcos would be smoking his pipe in the doorway, and nobody would be worrying yet because he had once said he was not afraid of anything, and everyone believed him a little longer than they should have. But he was here now, and the soup was good, and Elena was across the table from him turning a piece of bread over in her hands as if it held a story written in the crust.

"Did you really climb the hill in the dark?" she asked.

"I stopped at the top," he said. "The sun was going down."

Clara nodded, the way Marcos nodded sometimes, as if that explained things. "That's the rule then. The hill has rules."

"My grandfather gave it to me," [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] said. "With a rule about the sun. My father's rule, really."

"It's a good rule," Elena said. She set the bread down and picked up her soup bowl. "I found something. Do you want to see?"

She got up without waiting for an answer and went to a shelf above the old stove, where there were four books with broken spines and a shelf-worth of ordinary things: a brass key that opened nothing in this house, a bird's skull so small it fit in her palm, and a fossil.

It was smooth and sleeping, pale as a river stone, shaped like a spiral that had closed on itself a thousand years ago.

"I found it in the riverbed," Elena said. "Last spring. After the thaw."

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] pulled Captain Crunch out of his canvas bag, still wrapped in the handkerchief, and set it next to Elena's fossil on the kitchen table. His face was hot. He was about to have to say the name out loud, to a twelve-year-old girl he had only met an hour ago, and it was the most embarrassing thing that had ever happened to him.

"It's called Captain Crunch," he said quickly, as if speed would help. "I named it when I was eight. After a cereal."

Elena picked up her fossil and turned it over in her hands, slow and careful, the way she turned over everything. Then she picked up Captain Crunch and turned that over too, studying it the way a reader studies a page.

"That's a good name," she said. And she meant it. She didn't laugh—not in the bad way, not in any way at all. She just held both fossils, one in each hand, and looked at them as if she were reading two stories that fit together. "Mine doesn't have a name yet."

"You could name it," he said.

"Maybe," Elena said. "Maybe I'll wait and see what it tells me."

Clara had gone outside to tend the wall. Bruno's head was heavy on [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s foot, warm and solid. Through the kitchen window, the hill rose up in the blue-grey light, and the last of the sunset was fading fast, and he understood that he had to leave at first light because that was the rule, and because his plate was being kept warm, and because there were people somewhere learning what it meant to wait.

"We should sleep," Elena said, as if she had read the thought. "If you're going down in the morning."

The night was long and gentle. When the sky was still pale—earlier than [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] expected, earlier than the birds had finished their talking—Elena was already at the door, and Bruno was already standing beside her, and they began the walk down the hill together.

The path was safer in daylight. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could see every stone, could see where his feet were going, could see Bruno's scruffy back moving ahead of them both. The sun was rising at the foot of the valley, and it caught the stones of the path and the stones of the dry-stone wall that ran down the slope, and turned them gold. Bruno moved between them both, his tail high, quick and pleased, a sharp bark when the sun broke fully over the valley rim.

When they reached the bottom, where the hill met the lower path that led back to the village, Elena stopped and turned back toward the hill. She was looking up at it, and her hands were empty, but she was turning something over in her mind, [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could tell. The sun was catching the stones, catching her hair, and she was still looking back at the hill as if she were reading its story, as if the earth had given up more than just a fossil and now she was reading what it meant.
