# Chapter 1 — Ten Years Old

Leo woke before the cicadas stopped singing. The light through his window was already the color of honey, which meant it was late enough that the kitchen would be warm and his grandmother might have left honey on the table with the bread. He lay still for a moment, listening to the particular quiet of ten-year-old mornings — the kind of quiet that felt like it was waiting for something.

Bruno lifted his head from the foot of the bed, saw that Leo was awake, and remembered that he had been sleeping there without permission again. The small terrier scrambled into a tighter ball, but his tail kept moving, betraying him.

"You can stay," Leo said.

The kitchen was exactly as he'd pictured: honey on the table, bread still warm in its cloth, his mother already wearing the look she got on birthday mornings — the one that meant she had been awake for hours. His father was reading the newspaper but reading slowly, the kind of reading that meant he was waiting to look up at something.

"Ten," his father said, not looking away from the page. "Today you are ten."

Marcos arrived while Leo was still eating. His grandfather moved through the door the way he moved through everything, as though he had already walked this path a hundred times and knew exactly where every stone was. He carried a small package wrapped in brown paper, the kind of brown paper that looked like it had been chosen carefully rather than torn from whatever was nearest.

"The stones told me you were ready," Marcos said, which was what Marcos said about most things. He set the package on the table.

Inside was a notebook. Not a school notebook with lines that ruled your thinking into neat rows, but a leather-bound book with blank pages and a spine that creaked when Leo opened it. The leather was soft and darker on the edges where it had been handled, as though the book had been someone else's for a long time before becoming his.

"For seeing," Marcos said. He tapped his walking stick twice against the stone floor. "Not writing down the answers. Writing down the questions you couldn't ask anyone else."

Leo turned the first page slowly. It was empty and it felt important, like standing at the edge of something.

"There's a pencil," his mother said. "In the kitchen drawer. The sharp one we keep."

Bruno followed him to his room. The dog always followed him to his room, but today it felt intentional, as though Bruno understood that something had shifted in the house. Leo opened the window wider to let more light in, then climbed onto his chair to reach the shelf above his desk.

Captain Crunch sat where he always sat, fossil-hard and ancient and worn smooth by whatever water or time had touched it before Leo found it two summers ago on the beach at Cadaques. His father had laughed when he named it after the cereal with the cartoon pirate on the box, and then everyone had laughed, and then nobody had called it anything else. It was not a formal name, but it was the real one.

He placed the new notebook carefully beside the fossil, close enough that they looked as though they belonged together. The afternoon light coming through the window was different from the light he usually saw at this hour, or maybe the leather of the notebook was catching it and throwing it back in a way that made everything look sharper. He picked up Captain Crunch — the familiar weight of it, the smooth curve that felt like it had been made to fit his palm — and looked at it in this new brightness.

The light showed him something he hadn't quite seen before: a thin ridge that ran along one side, like the edge of a shell, like something that had once been alive and had folded itself into this shape. He turned it slowly. Maybe it had been a creature that curled up when it was afraid. He set it down gently and opened the notebook.

With the sharp pencil, he wrote the first question: "What shape was it?"

He did not write the answer. Marcos had been clear about that.

Bruno had found the cool corner of the room where the stone wall stayed cold even when the rest of the house burned with summer heat. He circled three times and collapsed onto the rough tile, his chin resting on his paws, watching Leo with the patient expression of a dog who knew there would be supper eventually.

At the kitchen table at six o'clock exactly, when the light had turned golden and starting to lean toward evening, his mother set the bread down and his father folded his newspaper. Leo's mother had made the soup that meant something was being celebrated, the one with tomatoes and basil and small pasta shapes, the one that took all morning.

"Your mother wants to know if you're planning to do something dangerous with your birthday," his father said.

"I'm ten," Leo said. "I'm not afraid of anything."

His father waited, the way adults do when they know you haven't finished speaking.

"I want to go to the hill," Leo said.

The spoon stopped in his mother's hand. Not for long — she was watching him carefully but not as though he'd said something impossible. Marcos, who had appeared at the table as he always did at supper time, looked at his own bowl.

"You're old enough," his father said. He cut his bread in half. "You'll go in the daylight. You'll be home before the sun touches the horizon. That is the only rule about the hill."

"The sun touches—" Leo started.

"When it is fully down," his father said, "the paths change. Distance becomes unreliable. You don't go learning a place in the dark." He put the bread in his mouth and chewed. His father was not a person who gave long speeches. When he spoke, it meant something. "When you are older and you've crossed it a hundred times in daylight, then you can think about learning it in the dark. For now: daylight. Home before the sun touches the horizon."

Bruno ate steadily from his own bowl under the table, an arrangement nobody had officially agreed to but everyone had understood for years. His tail thumped the floor in the rhythm of a dog who knew that supper meant eventually Leo would also be under the table, dropping pieces of bread.

Leo didn't answer. The rule was clear. The rule wasn't unreasonable — it was just that suddenly the hill was real in a way it hadn't been before, and real things that are allowed are somehow much more frightening than things that are forbidden.

He reached into his pocket where, without deciding to, he had put one of the stones from his collection that morning. It was a small smooth stone from the river, gray with a white stripe running through it, the kind that fit perfectly in the hollow of his palm. He turned it over and over while he ate, feeling its warmth under the kitchen light, while his father folded his newspaper again, and his mother watched him with the look that said she knew this day had meant something, and the stone turned, and turned, in his hands.
