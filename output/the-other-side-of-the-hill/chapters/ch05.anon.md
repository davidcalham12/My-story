# Chapter 5 — The Climb

The sun was high and the world was blue. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] and Bruno set out after breakfast while the air was still cool and the whole day lay ahead of them like a promise. The stone path was clear beneath his feet, the leather of his shoes worn smooth by his own walking, and Bruno trotted beside him with his nose down, reading the morning in the dust.

They climbed steadily. The village fell away below them, small and ordinary, rooftops and gardens and the ribbon of the stream. The pine shadows grew longer as they rose, and the air changed—thinner, clearer, the kind of air that made him feel he could see everything. He could see the fountain where Rosa said goodbye. He could see the ovens where she was working instead of being here. He had promised to tell her everything.

His legs began to ache. Not sharply yet, but a steady pulling in his thighs and a burning in his calves that sharpened with each step upward. Bruno felt it too; his trotting slowed, his tongue lolling in the afternoon heat. The sun that had felt cool in the morning was working harder now. Sweat stuck his shirt to his back. The higher they climbed, the smaller the world below became, until the village looked like something painted on paper, unreal and distant. His breath came harder. Each step demanded more thinking than the last—deciding whether to continue, whether his body could keep going. The path that had been clear began to ask questions.

Past the shepherd's markers, the path began to disappear.

The stones were still there, worn by feet and weather, but they came and went now, and between them was loose shale and low brush. He had to watch where he placed each step. Bruno was still beside him, but something in the dog's manner had changed. He stayed closer, his usual trotting urgency gone. When they paused in a stretch of deep shade beneath an overhang of rock, Bruno moved against [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s leg and whined—a thin, sharp sound that cut through the afternoon stillness.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] stopped. His breath was coming shorter than it should, his palms suddenly damp. The stones around him felt loud under his feet, as if they might give way. He turned smooth pebbles over in his hands, the way he did when thinking, and understood something true about himself:

It was not the dark he was afraid of.

"Bruno," he said aloud, and his voice cracked. "I don't know if I can—" He swallowed hard. His hand shook when he reached down to touch the dog's head. "What if I can't find the way back down from here? What if we get lost and I can't find the path and you're trusting me and I can't—I don't know the way."

The dog leaned against his shin. His small body was warm. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] thought of his mother, of his father, of the table where they would all sit at supper—and of the empty chair that would be his, the plate kept warm. He thought of Bruno's leash, which the dog had fetched himself on Day 2, which meant Bruno knew they were going somewhere that mattered.

He went on.

The climb sharpened. The fear did not leave—it sat in his ribs like something that had made a nest there—but he climbed anyway. One foot, then the other. Bruno stayed pressed against him, his breath quick and anxious, but forward.

And then, without warning, the ground leveled.

His breath rushed back in. The ache in his legs shifted, changed into something lighter. For a moment he stood still, chest heaving, and felt the fear drain out of him like water from cupped hands—not disappearing entirely, but settling into a smaller shape, something he could carry rather than something that was carrying him.

They stepped out into light and space. Before them lay a whole valley that did not exist from the village—had never been mentioned in all the village talk, never been real until this moment. There was a stream, silver in the failing light, and fields that descended to a stone house with a wall around it. There was a stream, silver in the afternoon sun, and fields that went down toward a stone house with a wall around it. The house was small and solid and real. In the yard, someone was standing with one hand over her eyes, looking up at the hill.

Looking at him.

She was a girl, perhaps twelve, with dark hair and a brown dress. She stood motionless, working something out, and then she called across the distance. Her voice reached him thinned and strange, as if traveling miles, though the valley was not wide—or was it? It sounded both nearer and farther than he could tell.

"Who are you?"

He took the first step down the slope toward her.

The sun touched the horizon.

The light changed in an instant. The crest behind him was still bright, but the valley below darkened all at once, and distance became unreliable. Things looked both nearer and farther than they truly were. He felt it in his chest—the rule arriving, the rule his father had given him, arriving with sudden force and weight. It did not say run. It said stop, wherever you are, when the sun touches the world's edge. Wait. Remain. Let daylight return. And that waiting is not a failure or a breaking of obedience. That is how you keep safe. That is the bravest thing.

He had not broken his father's rule. He was keeping it now.

From below, in the yard, Elena's voice reached him—stretched thin and strange by distance, sounding both very close and impossibly far. "You can't come down," she called, uncertainty bright in her voice. "Can you? Not safely now."

She was climbing toward him. He could see her now, moving up the slope with careful steps, taking her time, letting her feet find the way in the changing light. She moved as if she had walked this slope in darkness before, as if the dark was just another thing the land asked of her.

When she reached him, her breath was steady and her eyes serious, considering his face.

"Come on," she said simply. She bent and took Bruno by the collar, her hand gentle and certain. "We have to go to the house now. My grandmother—I'll need to tell her."

Together, the three of them descended toward the stone house with its lit windows, toward warmth and shelter and the smell of food cooking. Elena led them both toward the light.
