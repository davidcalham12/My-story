# Chapter 3 — The Hill Will Teach

Marcos was re-taping a seam when [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] found him in the garden.

The cardboard observatory stood where it had stood for two summers, near the back wall of the house, midnight blue fading to the color of old jeans where the afternoon shadow deepened. One corner of the seam had come loose where the rain had softened the cardboard, and Marcos was patient with it, running his palm across the joint, peeling back the tape slowly so as not to tear anything underneath. His walking stick leaned against the observatory wall within arm's reach. From inside, if you bent close, you could smell the dust that had collected there — a smell like pencil shavings and old boxes and secrets kept in the dark.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] had meant to ask last night, or this morning at breakfast, but the question had stuck in his throat each time. Now it came out rushed, while Marcos's hands were busy.

"What is on the other side of the hill?"

Marcos did not turn around. He smoothed the tape and reached for another strip from the roll in his lap, letting the question sit for a moment in the morning air while cicadas sang in the olive trees. The garden smelled of dust and wild rosemary and the particular warmth of stone that had been baking in the sun for hours already. A breeze moved through the olive leaves, and the cardboard observatory creaked very slightly, the sound of it like the settling of an old house.

"Why doesn't anyone talk about it?" [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] added, because the first answer hadn't come and he thought another question might hurry it along.

Bruno, who had followed [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] out from the house, had settled into the dust near the observatory's base and was watching something with great attention — a beetle, perhaps, or the shadow of a leaf shifting in the breeze. He circled three times before lying down, the way he did everywhere, as if he had to wind himself up before he could rest. His nose twitched at whatever story the dust held. Every now and then his tail gave a single happy thump.

"Come here," Marcos said.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] came. He watched his grandfather's hands move along the seam, sure and steady, the way they had moved when they built this thing together two summers ago, laughing at the ridiculous crooked tube they'd made at first, the one that pointed at nothing in the sky. Inside the observatory, if you bent to look through the cardboard tube, you could see stars pencilled in on the curved inner walls: Polaris, Orion, Cassiopeia, the names written in careful letters. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] had written them there when he was eight, before he understood constellations properly, and he had been wrong about some of them, and Marcos had never said so.

"Hand me that glass, would you?"

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] handed him the lens — the polished circle of glass that served as the telescope's eye. Marcos wiped it with the hem of his shirt, leaving a thumbprint smear, then wiped that away too, slowly, methodically, as if the lens were made of something that could break if you hurried. He was not doing any of this quickly. The morning was his, the tape was his, the lens was his. There was no rush in Marcos, and he moved through the world the way an old house moves through a storm — steadily, and certain it would still be standing when the wind stopped.

When he finally set the lens down and turned to look at [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] directly, his hand had already moved to the walking stick. He drew it toward him and rested it across his lap. There was a long pause — the kind Marcos made without apology, as if silence were a room he was walking through and there was no hurry to reach the other side.

Then he tapped the stick twice against the ground.

"When your father was ten," he said, "I took him to the far side of the town, to a place where there was a stone wall we wanted to see. He wanted to know how long it would take to get there, and whether we would see it clearly when we arrived, and what we would do when we got there." Marcos paused, tapping the stick once more against his palm, thoughtfully. "I told him the wall would teach him what he needed to know."

He stopped.

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] waited for the rest of the story, but there didn't seem to be one. There was no rest, no payoff, no proper ending. Marcos had tapped twice — that meant he was telling the truth. But it was the kind of truth that left a person standing with an empty cup, more thirsty than before.

"That's not an answer," [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] said, and even he could hear that his voice had gone a bit sharp, the way it did when he was frustrated with someone he loved. "That's not — you're doing the thing. The story thing."

Marcos's face creased at the corners, the way it did when he was trying not to smile.

"Yes," Marcos said.

"It's not fair."

"No. It isn't."

And that, somehow, was worse than if Marcos had defended himself or explained or tried to make it better. Bruno lifted his head from the dust, sensing the edge in the air the way dogs do, and trotted over to press his small warm weight against [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s leg. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]'s hand went down automatically to the scruffy fur, and after a moment he felt the tension begin to drain out of him — not because the question had been answered, but because Marcos was waiting, and Bruno was there, and the morning was still warm and wide and full of hours.

"Your father walked to that wall," Marcos said finally, his voice slower now, more careful. "And he climbed over it, and he found that on the far side there was nothing but another wall, and beyond that another field just like the one before. He came home disappointed. He thought he had wasted the day."

Marcos was quiet again, gathering the tape roll, smoothing the resealed seam with the heel of his hand, checking it twice.

"But he never asked me again to tell him what he would find before he walked somewhere new. That lesson was already his."

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] felt Bruno lean harder against his leg. Through the dust he could see the beetle the dog had been watching — a small green thing that had made its way to the observatory's shadow and seemed perfectly content to stay there in the cool, asking nothing of anyone, wanting nothing but the shade and whatever small life beetles want.

"So the hill will teach me," [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] said. It wasn't a question.

"The hill will teach you," Marcos agreed.

And [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] understood, with the kind of clarity that comes when you stop fighting against something and let it carry you, that this was all Marcos would say. It was all he would ever say, not because he was being cruel or secretive, but because some answers cannot be carried from one person to another like stones in a bag. They have to be walked toward and found. They have to be yours alone.

He looked at his hands, which were empty. The day stretched ahead of him, long and bright and full of hours before the sun would touch the horizon.
