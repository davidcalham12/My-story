# Chapter 9 — The Question Answered

The kitchen was the same as always—the long table, the light through the western window turning everything honey-colored, the smell of his mother's bread and olive oil. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] sat in his regular place, still dusty from the hill, and nobody said anything about the dirt on the chair. His mother had filled his plate without asking what he wanted. His father sat across from him the way he always did. Marcos was in the corner chair, the one that faced both the table and the door, watching the evening come in.

They ate without much talking at first—just the sound of forks, the clink of water glasses, his mother asking his father something about the shutters that needed fixing. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] was hungrier than he had ever been. Everything tasted like itself: the bread was bread, salt was salt, the cheese was exactly what cheese was supposed to be. He thought about Clara's kitchen, about the meal there that morning—Elena handing him a thick cup of milk, Clara setting down a plate without fuss—and then he thought about sitting in this kitchen instead, and how it was the same shape of kindness but different in its details, in the way his mother folded her napkin and the particular creak of this chair under his weight.

"You'll want to tell Rosa everything," his mother said. Not a question.

"She won't forgive me if I don't," he said.

His father laughed—a real laugh, not a polite one. "She had a point about the feast ovens keeping her here."

[NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] nodded and kept eating. The sun was lower now, the light different. He thought about yesterday, about the moment the sun had touched the horizon and he had understood, really understood, that he could not go further down the hill into the dark. It had been frightening—that moment when the rules and the fear had suddenly meant the same thing. But then Clara had opened the door, and Elena had been there, and fear had become something else. A thing that happened and then was over. The dark had come all around him in that house, in that bed that wasn't his bed, and it had not swallowed him. Bruno had slept at the foot of it, breathing steady. And when the light came back, [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] was still himself, unchanged in any way that mattered.

He lifted his glass and drank. The water was cold. His father was talking about the weather, about whether the heat would break before September. His mother was not really listening; she was watching [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] in that way she had that made him feel known. Marcos was silent in his corner, one hand resting on his walking stick, his eyes on [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO]. The rhythm of supper. The rhythm that had held his place all night while he was gone.

After they had eaten, after his mother had cleared the plates and brought out the figs they always had at this hour, [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] excused himself. His mother nodded. Nobody asked where he was going. He climbed the stairs to his room in the half-dark—twilight now, the last real light draining out of the day—and closed the door quietly.

Bruno was already on the bed, waiting. The dog lifted his head when [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] came in, and when the boy lay down and turned toward the wall, Bruno jumped down and circled. Once, twice, three times—that was his way, the habit of settling that [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] had never thought much about until now. But this time, when Bruno circled, there was no tension in it. He was circling because that was what Bruno did, his body loose and easy, the way he always settled in to sleep. When he lay down at the foot of the bed, he sighed—a small dog sigh, almost content—and rested his chin on his paws.

When Bruno lay down at the foot of the bed, [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] could feel the difference right away. His tail was straight, his ears forward, not pinned back. And his whole body—it wasn't coiled and ready anymore, waiting for something to happen. He was just Bruno, settling in to sleep the way he always did when nothing was wrong. The night was the same as any other night for him. So it was the same for [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] too.

The light went on fading. His room became shades of grey and then something softer than that, something between blue and nothing. On the shelf, Captain Crunch caught what little light was left and seemed to glow—though it was only the pale limestone picking up the last glow from the western sky. [NOMBRE_ANONIMIZADO] thought about Elena's fossil on Clara's shelf, the spiral pale as bone, the way they had stood together looking at both of them, at the two things pulled from the earth that neither of them had made and both of them had kept.

He reached for his notebook. The leather one Marcos had given him, that first morning when he turned ten. He had asked a question on the first page and never gone back to it. Now he opened to a fresh page and found the pencil in the spine where he always kept it. The light was almost gone but there was enough, there was enough for this. He pressed the pencil to paper and began to write, the letters small and careful:

*Elena has a fossil too. We are the same kind of person.*

The pencil moved across the page and the words took their place, one after the other, true and solid on the paper, exactly as he had meant them to be.
