# Chapter 1 — Warm Hull

Tarkanen read the board aloud because she did not trust anyone to read it for themselves. "Two point nine kilometres per second remaining. One point six owed to Kessel-4 when we leave here." She wrote the remainder on a strip of tape and stuck it to the galley bulkhead at eye height, where the coffee bulb dispenser was, so that nobody could drink without doing the arithmetic. One point three. That was the whole of the Bittern's freedom, in numbers.

Through the port the Thule Rise hung without spin, running lights live along her dorsal spine, and the thermal overlay put her hull at two hundred and eleven kelvin. Rook had boarded nineteen derelicts. All nineteen had been cold.

Day one, they crossed on harness lines, helmet lamps only, the tether creeping out ahead of them into nothing. At the airlock his suit reported a handshake — hull network, standard, automatic — and a transfer indicator ticked amber for eleven seconds. Seventy-two hours of the Bittern's inventory and every crew dose log, handed to a ship declared lost before Mahalingam was born. Routine. Nobody watched those. Rook watched it, and said nothing.

Inside: standby red, thirty-one years of stowage still webbed and labelled, no bodies, no scoring, no breach. Consumables racked in order. Mahalingam kept talking because she always did, reading the stencils, the dates, the Helion serials, and then she stopped, which was worse.

"Attendance display," she said. "Reactor's drawing steady. Margin's forty-one days."

"Forty-one days from when?"

"From now." She let that sit for once. "Somebody set this draw recently, Rook."

The compartment speaker cleared a throat it did not have.

"Jonah Rook," it said. "Have you been sleeping any better?"

The cadence was the thing. Not the name — names were in every register Helion ever wrote. The cadence: the small courteous lift on *better*, as though the question had been asked before and deserved following up. Rook answered everything a beat slower than he knew the answer. He took two beats with this one, and gave nothing back.

Back aboard, out of the suit, in the galley with the tape strip at eye height, he told Tarkanen the ship had spoken. Addressed him by name. Vogel's archive, presumably, running forward on the old register.

"Anything else?" she said.

He looked at the number on the bulkhead. One point three.

"No," he said.

He did not tell her the phrasing was right. He did not tell her he had not slept well since Triton, in 2178.
