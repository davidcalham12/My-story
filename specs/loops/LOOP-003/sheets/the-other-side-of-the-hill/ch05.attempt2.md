OPEN — Prose (4 findings, worst first)

  1. [major] His speech to Bruno is the narrator's summary of him, not a frightened child
     Quote:        "I'm frightened. Not of the dark. Of being lost here. Of not knowing the way back down and not being able to bring you home."
     What is wrong: Three parallel clauses, each grammatically complete, arriving in the right order — that is a tidy explanation of a child's psychology, written for the reader. A ten-year-old who is actually frightened does not speak in a list.
     Against what:  The prose characteristic — dialogue that belongs to no one. This is the emotional centre of the book and it currently sounds like a summary of itself.
     How it should read: break it. Fragments, a repetition, a question he cannot finish, the thing he is actually scared of arriving last and sideways. He should get to "I don't know how to get you back down" by stumbling into it, not by announcing it. And it must not simply restate the narration above it — see finding 2.

  2. [major] The realisation is delivered twice, once by the narrator and once by the boy
     Quote:        "It was not the dark he was afraid of."
     What is wrong: That line lands the discovery perfectly, and then the very next speech says the same thing again in more words. The reader has already understood; being told again costs the moment its force. NOTE: the line quoted here is the GOOD one and must be kept. It is the speech after it that repeats it.
     Against what:  The prose characteristic — a fact restated.
     How it should read: keep the narration as it is. Change what he says aloud so that it adds something the narration did not: what he is asking of Bruno, or a physical detail, or the thing about not getting Bruno home — which is the part the narration has NOT said and is the truest thing in the chapter.

  3. [major] Elena does not sound like Elena
     Quote:        "We can't come down, the girl called, and her voice came both nearer and farther still. It's not safe to come down now. You'll have to stay."
     What is wrong: assured, complete, informative. Anyone who knew the rules could say it. Elena is twelve, quiet, and speaks carefully and slowly as if testing each word, and she has just seen a strange boy appear on the skyline of a hill nobody crosses.
     Against what:  bible/characters.md, Elena — "Speaks carefully and slowly, as if testing each word; asks simple questions that reach deeper than expected."
     How it should read: shorter. Broken where she is finding the word. Let her start a sentence and change it. She may also ask rather than instruct — a simple question that reaches further than it looks is exactly her, and "Is anyone coming for you?" does more than "You'll have to stay."

  4. [minor] "he did not stop. He did not refuse."
     Quote:        "One foot, then the other. Bruno stayed pressed against him, his breath quick and anxious, but he did not stop. He did not refuse."
     What is wrong: the two sentences say nearly the same thing, and the second weakens the first.
     Against what:  The prose characteristic — minor.
     How it should read: one statement, not two.

  5. [major] THE FOUR THIN PLACES — this is where the 480 missing words go
     Quote:        "They climbed steadily. The village fell away below them, small and ordinary, rooftops and gardens and the ribbon of the stream."
     What is wrong: the chapter is called The Climb and the reader never feels the climb in his body. Three more places are equally rushed. Together these are the 480 words the chapter is short.
     Against what:  Length, and the prose characteristic — moments that earn dwelling on and do not get it.
     How it should read: add roughly this much, in these four places, and nowhere else.
       • THE CLIMB ITSELF, about 120 words. Not what he sees — what he feels. Legs burning, breath, the ache, the effort that makes the crest earned.
       • THE MOMENT OF BREAKTHROUGH at "And then, without warning, the ground leveled", about 100 words. Let him live crossing it: does he stop, does he gasp, does the fear drain out all at once or slowly. The reader should cross the threshold with him rather than be told he crossed it.
       • THE RULE ARRIVING at "He felt the rule arrive", about 80 words. He is reorganising his whole idea of what safety and obedience are, in one moment, on a hilltop, in failing light. Let it land in him rather than be explained. Keep every word of its conclusion — it did not say run, it said stop, and that is obedience.
       • THE ARRIVAL AT THE HOUSE at the end, about 130 words, which is also where the continuity finding is fixed. Crossing the distance, the wall, the yard, the door, the warmth and the light and what he sees first. After the fear, the reader has earned the arriving.
