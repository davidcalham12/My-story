  1. [high] The field book is dated a day behind
     Quote:        "wrote *17 May, cont.* at the head of a clean leaf"
     What is wrong: This evening is the eighth day of the survey, and the eighth day is Thursday the 18th of May. The 17th was the day before — the Wednesday he came back over the ford and ran the line four times, and it is already used. He is starting a clean leaf for the eleven pages of the day he has just worked, so the head of that leaf is today's date. A surveyor misdating his own leaf is not a small slip here: this book's dates are the only thing standing between the reader and chaos, and this is the chapter where the book turns out to contain an entry he has no memory of making. The page must be beyond reproach on the very evening before it betrays him.
     Against what:  bible/timeline.md — the first day of the survey is Thursday 11 May 1843, which makes the eighth day Thursday 18 May and the ninth Friday 19 May. The entry he finds at the end of this chapter is the ninth day's.
     How it should read: The head of the leaf carries the eighteenth of May. If "cont." is to stay, it can only mean he is continuing a leaf already begun that same evening; otherwise drop it. Nothing else in the sentence changes — he still hears the words go past him like a cart in the lane.

     Against what:  bible/timeline.md — the fifth day is the solo re-measurement that came back identical; the sixth is the ride to Ingleford; the seventh is the return and the four runs.
     How it should read: Either the number becomes three, or the sentence makes plain that he is counting today's unclosed sight as the fourth. Either is fine; what is not fine is a figure the reader cannot reconstruct. Keep the weariness of the line exactly as it is.
