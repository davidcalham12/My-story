OPEN — Science (1 finding) and Continuity (2 findings), worst first
  1. [high] A line the vicar reads aloud can be turned into a distance
     Quote:        "From the thorn, a quarter of an hour at a woman's pace, to the intake wall that Joseph Dent kept."
     What is wrong: A quarter of an hour is a clock and "at a woman's pace" is a rate, and a clock times a rate is a distance. The man reading it aloud to sixty people is dale-born, and the rule forbids him a distance in his own words **or read off a page** — so if that line holds a distance, he has just said one to the whole parish, and the chapter's own defence collapses three sentences later where the narration says there was "not once in two hours a distance". It also undoes the best idea in the chapter: that a century and a half of hands wrote the bound this way **precisely so that it could be said out loud by people who cannot say a distance**. One line in the quoted passage is not of that kind, and it is the one the reader can do arithmetic on.
     Against what:  bible/world.md, the speech rule — nothing dale-born hands have written holds a distance to read; the perambulation book keeps the bound in durations and the names of dead men.
     How it should read: A duration tied to an act or a person, with no rate attached — the shape of the line beside it, which is safe because "the time it takes to say the Creed twice" cannot be converted by anybody. Keep the thorn, keep a woman in it if you want her, and keep the intake wall that Joseph Dent kept. Change only the measure between them.

  2. [high] The custom has aged ten years since it was last counted
     Quote:        "A hundred and fifty years of hands had kept it so."
     What is wrong: The parish book's earliest hand is dated 1703 and this is 1843, which is a hundred and forty years, and a hundred and forty is the figure the book has already been given. This is the closing chapter and this number is the measure of the thing the whole story is about — how long the dale has kept itself off paper. It cannot drift by a decade on the last page.
     Against what:  bible/timeline.md — the survey is of May 1843; and the parish book's earliest hand is dated 1703. The arithmetic gives a hundred and forty.
     How it should read: A hundred and forty years. Nothing else in the sentence changes.

  3. [medium] The ford stone is said to match a figure that was never taken
     Quote:        "Four feet two inches and a half above the water: exactly what it had given him on his first day, exactly what it would give anybody."
     What is wrong: On the day he came in over the ford the surveyor did not measure the stone's height. He looked at it and thought it "perhaps four feet out of the water" — an eyeballed impression, not a figure. So there is no first-day number for four feet two and a half to be *exactly* equal to, and a reader who remembers the first page will notice the book handing itself a proof it did not earn. The point is still available and is better honestly made: the stone is one of the three that hold, and it gives the same answer to everybody who ever asks it.
     Against what:  bible/world.md, the rule of the three constants — the ford stone measures the same for everyone in every survey ever made. That is what is true here, and it does not need a spurious first-day figure to carry it.
     How it should read: Let the match be with his rough first impression rather than with a figure — as near as his first guess had put it, and exactly what it would give anybody who ever asked it. Keep the measured four feet two and a half; keep the last clause about the constants not caring who holds the chain, which is right.
